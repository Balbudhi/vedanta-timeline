# Śambara’s artificial warriors

## Why this is the “robot” story

Swami Venkatesananda’s *Vasiṣṭha’s Yoga* calls Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa “robot-like.” That phrase successfully points to the strangeness of the episode, but it is not the translation of one Sanskrit word. It compresses a sequence of descriptions in *Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha*, Sthiti-prakaraṇa 4.25.34–40. The text calls the three *kṛtrima* (constructed or artificial), says that their actions proceed through an *antaś-codaya* (an inward prompting), and compares their movement to the limbs of children who are half asleep. At the same time, it says that *cetanā* and *cit* are present. “Robot” is therefore a functional analogy, not an assertion that they are inert mechanisms.

This article follows the narrative boundary of Venkatesananda’s concise chapter, but the translation below has been made independently from the Sanskrit. The Sanskrit narrative itself runs for 294 verses, 4.25.6–4.32.26. Much of that expansion is ornate battle poetry and repeated instruction. The retelling preserves every turn in the story while compressing those extended descriptions; each paragraph gives its Sanskrit range so the compression remains visible. The six verses on the making and operation of the artificial warriors are presented afterward without compression, word by word.

## The story, retranslated from the Sanskrit

Vasiṣṭha has warned Rāma not to follow the *nyāya*, the instructive pattern, of Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa. Rāma asks what that pattern is. Vasiṣṭha answers that he should hear both it and the contrasting steadfastness of Bhīma, Bhāsa, and Dṛḍha, and then act as he sees fit. (4.25.1–6)

In a cavern of Pātāla lived Śambara, lord of the daityas and an ocean of the jewel of *māyā*. His domain was itself a work of construction: aerial cities and gardens, artificial suns and moons, lotuses made from stone, jeweled birds, golden trees, cold fire, mechanical beings, and pleasure grounds surpassing those of the gods. His wealth filled the three worlds; his command was honored by the daitya lords; his army was immense. (4.25.7–21)

Whenever Śambara slept or traveled elsewhere, the gods found an opening and destroyed his troops. He appointed commanders; the gods killed them. He appointed others; the same thing happened. In anger he invaded heaven, but the gods concealed themselves through their own *māyā*. Finding heaven empty, he burned the cities of its guardians, carried away what pleased him, and returned home. Yet each new commander he made was again killed. Unable to find the gods even after searching the three worlds, Śambara burned with rage. (4.25.22–33)

He then made three terrible, immensely powerful asuras by *māyā* to protect his army. They were named Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa. No previous action belonged to them, and no prior *vāsanā* had been accumulated. They did only the action that came before them. The text describes a slight, insubstantial, not-yet-nourished and *kṛtrima* portion of action-life and mind-division arising in them as an inward prompting. Their action continued in a blind succession, by coincidence rather than by a remembered project. Like half-sleeping children whose own limbs merely move, they acted without *vāsanā* and without *ātma-abhimāna*, the taking or measuring of something as oneself. Śambara rejoiced: an army protected by these *māyā*-made asuras would be stable. (4.25.34–44)

Śambara sent the army, guarded by the three, up from Pātāla. The armies of gods and daityas met. The Sanskrit devotes almost an entire chapter to the battle: hand-to-hand blows, weapons and counter-weapons, mountains used as missiles, rivers of blood, cities and forests destroyed, bodies falling through the sky, light and darkness alternating as weapons made suns, storms, fire, and flood. Dāma bound masses of gods; Vyāla crushed their dwellings; Kaṭa exhausted them in combat. The gods fled and disappeared. The three returned to their maker, pleased, because no enemy remained visible. (4.26.1–4.27.8)

Defeated, the gods went to Brahmā and described Śambara’s work and the three commanders. Brahmā told them that Śambara’s death at Hari’s hand lay far in the future. For the present they should fight the three by strategy: engage them repeatedly and then flee. Habitual fighting would cause the flash of *ahaṃkāra* to appear in them as an image appears in a mirror. Once they had taken up *vāsanā*, they would be caught like birds in a net and could be conquered. Without it, pleasure and pain did not turn them aside and they were almost impossible to defeat. The gods’ task was not primarily to overpower their weapons but to produce in them the formations “I am this” and “this is mine.” (4.27.9–41)

The gods rested and then renewed the war. Again the poem expands the fighting into a world-sized storm of weapons, mountains, oceans, serpents, birds, fire, and bodies. Through retreat, concealment, renewed attack, truce, dispute, courage, fear, and every other tactic, the gods kept the conflict going for many years. Repetition did what force could not. Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa came to take up the stance “I.” From “I” came “my life” and “my wealth”; from these came hope, possessiveness, attachment to the body, food, drink, partners, and pleasure. They began to wish that their bodies would remain and that wealth would give them happiness. Courage vanished. The thought “we shall die” entered them. (4.28.1–4.29.18)

The gods attacked the three after this change. Now the commanders could not strike the warrior before them. Once fear of death had made the preservation of the body their single object, they became ordinary fighters. They fled. Their army broke with them, and the defeated daityas fell across mountains, seas, forests, towns, and distant regions. (4.29.19–34)

Śambara raged when he learned that his army had been destroyed and demanded to know where Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa had gone. Afraid of him, they fled to the seventh Pātāla, where Yama’s attendants gave each of them protection and a young woman. They lived there for ten thousand years, taking up ever more *vāsanā*: “this woman is mine,” “this child is mine,” “I am the master.” When Yama once arrived without the marks by which they would recognize him, they failed to honor him. At a movement of his brow they were cast into burning hells together with their families. Their own *vāsanā* then carried them through births as servants, hunters, crows, vultures, parrots, pigs, sheep, insects, and many other forms. At the time of Vasiṣṭha’s telling, they were fish in a shrinking, muddy forest pool in Kashmir, neither properly living nor dying. (4.30.1–18)

Vasiṣṭha draws the lesson sharply. The commanders who had routed the gods became exhausted fish; the distance between those conditions was made by *ahaṃkāra* and *vāsanā*. Rāma then asks a harder question. If the three were *māyā*-made and therefore not independently established, how did they come to have an apparently real continuity like ours? Vasiṣṭha answers by refusing the privilege hidden in the question: Dāma and the others are not uniquely unreal. “We,” gods, asuras, and all appearances arise in the same field. A formation appears according to the way *cit* apprehends itself; the reality of *cit* lends apparent stability to the formation without making the formation independently established. (4.31.1–48)

Rāma asks how the three will be released. Vasiṣṭha says that Yama had already declared the condition: they will be free when they are separated and hear their own story. After further animal births, the three fish will become waterbirds and eventually part. In a future city called Adhiṣṭhāna in Kashmir, Vyāla will live as a small bird in a high building on Pradyumna peak. Dāma will be a mosquito in a crack in a palace pillar. Kaṭa will be a caged bird in the house of the minister Narasiṃha. The minister will recite in verses the story of Dāma, Vyāla, and Kaṭa. Kaṭa will hear and remember the source of his apparent identity, and will reach cessation. The same story will later be heard by Vyāla and, incidentally, by Dāma; each will reach cessation in turn. (4.32.1–25)

Vasiṣṭha closes the narrative: this is the complete sequence of the story of Dāma and the others. *Māyā*, empty of independent foundation yet intensely radiant in appearance, makes those who have not discerned it wander as the conviction of water in a mirage does. (4.32.26–28)

## Is “robot” a good translation?

It is a good analogy if it remains visibly an analogy. Three features support it.

First, *kṛtrima* is unambiguous at the relevant level: made, constructed, artificial, as opposed to what has arisen through an inherited history. Second, *yathā-prāpta-eka-kartāraḥ* makes their agency purely occurrent: they are doers of the one thing that has presented itself, not agents pursuing a remembered personal project. Third, their action proceeds by *andha-pāramparya*, a blind sequence, and by an inward *codaya*, a prompting or impulse. The comparison with a half-asleep child makes the intended distinction bodily and functional.

But “robot” becomes a bad translation if it smuggles in the modern opposition between machine and consciousness. The verse does not call the three inert. It explicitly characterizes them through *cetanā* and through a *parispanda* of *cit-mātra*. What they initially lack is not sentience but an accumulated history of appropriation: prior *karma*, *vāsanā*, and *ātma-abhimāna*. They can perceive, strike, and respond. They do not yet convert action into “my action,” the body into “my body,” or continuance into “my life.”

The story’s experiment is therefore more exact than “can a machine think?” Śambara constructs capable responsive agents without autobiographical investment. The gods defeat them by manufacturing that investment. Habit produces *ahaṃkāra*; *ahaṃkāra* produces “mine”; “mine” produces preservation, fear, and finally vulnerability. The nearest modern gloss is not simply “robots,” but “artificial agents before a self-model has sedimented.” That longer phrase is less memorable than Venkatesananda’s, but it is closer to what the Sanskrit actually stages.

The Sanskrit is cited from the repository witness `data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/yogavasistha_sanskritsahitya.txt` at Sthiti-prakaraṇa 4.25.6–4.32.26. A local Muktābodha Yoga-Vāsiṣṭha Part 4 witness has been identified for the eventual second-witness collation, but this in-progress article does not claim that collation as complete. The English edition used only to identify the remembered story is Swami Venkatesananda, *Vasiṣṭha’s Yoga* (State University of New York Press, 1993), chapter “The Story of Dāma, Vyāla and Kaṭa,” pp. 165–170. Its wording has not been used as the translation base.

The public close reading is deliberately limited to 4.25.34 and 4.25.36–40, the closed six-verse population responsible for the “robot-like” interpretation. The wider story is translated in locus-marked compression because its 294-verse Sanskrit form is far longer than the concise English chapter. The grammatical records derive verbal roots from the local Pāṇinian Dhātupāṭha witness; the unfinished Prakriya interpreter and generated parses were not used.
