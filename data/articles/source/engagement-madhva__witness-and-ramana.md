# Madhva’s Witness and Ramaṇa’s ‘I’

## The question

The relevant issue is not whether the embodied person, as such, is Viṣṇu.
Madhva explicitly rejects that identification; Ramaṇa's inquiry likewise does
not ratify the body-mind as a separately divine person. The issue is whether a purified witness remains an
irreducibly individual conscious reality, distinct from Īśvara even in
liberation, or whether the very localization that makes a witness a *jīva*
has to end. Madhva and Ramaṇa give unusually direct, incompatible answers.

This note uses Madhva's primary texts and Ramaṇa's Sanskrit *Upadeśa Sāram*
first. *Nāṉ Yār?* is important corroboration, but its early answers and later
recensions need to be distinguished; the Ramanasramam edition describes its
question-and-answer material as recorded by Sivaprakasam Pillai. It is not
used here to override Ramaṇa's composed Sanskrit verse text.

## 1. What Madhva means by a pure witness

In *Anuvyākhyāna* 2.3.63–66, Madhva argues that error and non-error are known
by the witness, that the distinctions at issue are objects of witness-cognition
(*sākṣi-pratyaya-gocarāḥ*), and that practical life presupposes a
non-defective witness. He then concludes:

> *śuddhaḥ sākṣī yadā siddho duḥkhitvaṃ vāryate katham /
> upajīvyapramāṇaṃ tad bhedagrāhakam eva hi /
> ato jīveśayor bhedaḥ śrutisāmarthyasusthiraḥ //*

> “Once a pure witness is established, how can the state of being a sufferer be excluded? For the
> means of knowledge on which [one] relies apprehends difference itself.
> Therefore difference between jīva and Lord is firmly established by the
> force of scripture.”

The argument has three linked commitments.

1. **The witness is not merely the body-mind agent.** Madhva introduces it as
   *śuddha*, “pure,” and *nirdoṣa*, “non-defective.” The empirical person may
   be mistaken, but the witnessing knower required for certifying mistake is
   not thereby discarded. The verses do not grammatically identify *sākṣī*
   with *jīva* in a bare equation; rather, Madhva's conclusion—*jīveśayoḥ
   bhedaḥ*—places that non-defective witness within the final, differentiated
   jīva–Īśvara order.

2. **Difference is not merely an object within an otherwise nondual witness.**
   *Bheda-grāhaka* is a straightforward agentive compound: that which
   apprehends difference. Madhva makes this difference-cognition part of the
   evidence Advaita itself would have to rely upon.

3. **The witness is a *jīva* in final ontology.** The conclusion is explicitly
   *jīva–īśa-yoḥ bhedaḥ*. In the immediately following verses Madhva allows
   *abheda* language because of similarity, dependence, or primacy, but denies
   *svarūpa-a-bhidām*—non-distinction of essential nature. Here *a-bhidām* is
   the privative of *bhidā* (“difference”), not the unrelated word *abhidhā*
   (“denotation”). Verse 2.3.67 adds that qualities beginning with
   consciousness and bliss remain even in liberation. The liberated subject is
   therefore not a body-bound ego, but it is still a real, qualified and
   unequal jīva.

“Limited witness” needs care here. The cited argument does not call the
liberated witness spatially minute, and it would be an error to infer that
from Madhva's use of *aṇu* in every context. For example, *Anuvyākhyāna*
2.2.250 occurs inside a polemic against a Jain account of a self that changes
with differently sized bodies; its *aṇu-dehasya jīvasya* is not a standalone
definition of Madhva's final witness. The limitation established in the
present argument is instead **essential difference and dependence**:
*jīva–īśvara-bheda* is held to be *svarūpa* difference, not a bodily or
adjunct-produced boundary.

The first-person profile of that difference is explicit just before the
*sākṣī* argument. Madhva writes: *kiñcitkartā ca duḥkhīti sarvair eva
anubhūyate*—“all experience [the self] as an agent of something limited and as
a sufferer”—then contrasts this with the Lord as *sarvajña* and *sarvaśakti*
(2.3.50–51). The suffering belongs to the bound condition; Madhva does not
thereby call suffering a final property of the liberated. But the argument
uses direct self-experience to establish difference and then refuses to make
that difference adjunct-produced in liberation.

The same conclusion is explicit for liberation. In *Tattvodyota*, Madhva says
that if difference between the liberated and unliberated were absent, the
liberated would be in saṃsāra or the bound would be liberated; if difference
were made by ignorance, Īśvara would experience unity with the suffering
transmigrator. He concludes: *anaupādhikatvād eva bhedasya na
muktasyeśvaraikyam*—because difference is not produced by adjuncts, the
liberated does not have identity with Īśvara.

This is not a dismissal of the body-bound ego. Madhva's final subject is the
liberated, pure, difference-cognizing *jīva*. His view is asymmetrical—Viṣṇu
alone is independent—but asymmetry does not become non-difference.

## 2. Ramaṇa’s “I” is not Madhva’s final witness

Ramaṇa's *Upadeśa Sāram* turns on a distinction that Madhva cannot grant. Its
verses 19–21 do **not** ask an individual witness to recognize its permanent
limits. They ask where the arising “I” comes from, say that this “I” falls in
self-investigation, and identify the unperishing reality with what “I” truly
denotes even when the *aham* has subsided:

> *aham ayaṃ kuto bhavati chinvataḥ / ayi pataty ahaṃ nija-vicāraṇam //*

> “When one investigates, ‘From where does this I arise?’, the I falls: that
> itself is self-investigation.”

> *ahami nāśabhāji aham-aham-tayā / sphurati hṛt svayaṃ parama-pūrṇa-sat //*

> “When the *aham* has undergone destruction, the Heart itself shines as
> ‘I-I,’ the supreme, complete Being.”

The syntax matters. *ahami* is locative singular (“when/in the I”);
*nāśa-bhāji* qualifies it as undergoing destruction; *sphurati* has *hṛt*
(“Heart”) as its subject. This is not a statement that a surviving individual
ego obtains a new object of knowledge. The verse distinguishes the perishing
*aham* from self-shining Being.

Ramaṇa's Tamil *Uḷḷadu Nāṟpadu* gives the “I” a sharply different final
description from Madhva's. It does not replace the body with a more refined,
bounded subject. Verse 17 contrasts the unrealized “I” as body-measured
(*uḍal aḷavē*) with the realized “I” as shining “without limit” (*ellai aṟa
tāṉ oḷirum nāṉ*). This is a Tamil, not a Sanskrit, text, so Pāṇinian derivation
is not the relevant grammar. The verse rules out a merely body-bounded
realization of “I,” without by itself supplying a full theory of Īśvara or of
subjecthood.

Verse 24 says that a body-sized “I” arises between the inert body and Sat-Cit,
and identifies that arising knot as *cit-jaḍa-giranthi*, bondage, *jīvan*,
subtle body, ego, saṃsāra, and mind.

The verse’s list is programmatic. Here *jīvaṉ* is not named as a purified
individual witness that remains after correction; it is one designation for
the body-measuring, arising knot. This is why the disagreement with Madhva
cannot be reduced to a dispute about whether the empirical ego is God. Madhva
protects a pure witness from reduction to that ego; Ramaṇa places the
individualized *jīva* in the same analytic sequence as the ego-knot that
inquiry dissolves. This establishes a conflict in the two uses of *jīva*; it
does not by itself settle which metaphysical analysis is right.

Verses 26, 37, and 40 make the same boundary sharper. Verse 26 says that if
the ego (*ahandai*) exists, everything exists; if it does not, everything does
not, so investigating it is the letting-go of everything. Verse 37 rejects the
formula “duality in practice, nonduality in attainment” as an adequate final
account, using the tenth-man analogy: the seeker and the found one are not two
different selves. Verse 40 refuses to make liberation a third predicate among
form, formlessness, and form-and-formlessness: liberation is the destruction
of the ego-form that distinguishes those alternatives.

This also imposes a limit on an overly easy use of Ramaṇa for a positive
post-realisation phenomenology of forms. *Uḷḷadu Nāṟpadu* 4 asks: if oneself is
a form, the world and God are likewise forms; if oneself is not a form, who
sees their forms? It calls the true eye the “endless eye.” The point is not
that ordinary form-perception never occurs in experience; it is that it belongs
to the ego's form-bound standpoint and cannot be a predicate of the final Self
as such. Ramaṇa thus strongly supports non-localized Selfhood, but his primary
verse text cannot simply be cited as proof that differentiated forms remain
present to final awareness in the same sense.

Verse 24 then names the precise jīva–Īśvara difference that Madhva makes
final:

> *īśa-jīvayor veṣa-dhī-bhidā / sat-svabhāvato vastu kevalam //*

> “Between Īśvara and jīva there is difference of appearance and cognition;
> in their nature as Being, the reality is one alone.”

*īśa-jīvayoḥ* is genitive dual (“of/between Īśvara and jīva”);
*veṣa-dhī-bhidā* predicates a difference pertaining to guise/appearance
(*veṣa*) and cognition or intelligence (*dhī*); *sat-svabhāvataḥ* is an
ablative-form adverb, “from the standpoint of nature as Being”; and *vastu
kevalam* is neuter singular, “one reality only.” The verse does not deny that
the difference is experienced. It denies that it is difference in the final
nature of the reality.

Verse 25 makes the consequence explicit: *veṣa-hānataḥ svātma-darśanam /
īśa-darśanaṃ svātma-rūpataḥ*—on the removal of the guise/adjunct, seeing one's
own Self is seeing Īśvara, because Īśvara has the form/nature of one's own
Self. Verse 26 says that abiding as the Self is its own seeing, because the
Self is nondual. Verse 27 rejects a residual knower–known structure:

> *jñāna-varjitājñāna-hīna-cit / jñānam asti kiṃ jñātum antaram //*

> “Consciousness devoid of knowledge and free of ignorance—is
> there another thing to be known?”

No English rendering should hide the limit of these verses. They do not give a
complete theory of every empirical or devotional relation. They do settle the
narrower point needed here: Ramaṇa does not treat jīva–Īśvara difference as
final in *sat-svabhāva*, and he treats the arising “I”/*jīvan* as what inquiry
brings to an end.

## 3. The direct collision

The positions can now be stated without replacing either thinker’s terms.

| Question | Madhva | Ramaṇa |
|---|---|---|
| What survives correction of bodily ego? | A pure, non-defective, liberated *jīva*-witness, whose essential qualities remain. | Not the arising *aham*; it falls, while self-shining Being remains. In *Uḷḷadu* 17, the realized “I” is not body-bounded. |
| Is jīva–Īśvara difference final? | Yes: not adjunct-produced; it remains in liberation. | No: difference is *veṣa-dhī-bhidā*; in Being the reality is one. |
| What is direct realization? | Direct realization of Viṣṇu that preserves the knower–Lord difference. | Self-abidance in which no second knowable remains. |

Madhva therefore challenges the user's non-objectifying Advaita at its decisive
point. His argument is not answered by saying, “the empirical ego is not
Brahman,” because his final witness is already supposed to be free of that
mistake. Conversely, Ramaṇa is not answered merely by repeating that suffering
is known by a witness: he treats the arising *aham* as what must cease, rather
than granting Madhva's premise that the witness is a final individual entity.

Madhva's objection becomes a genuine philosophical challenge: why does the
cessation of the *aham* not erase the very witness needed to distinguish error
from truth? Ramaṇa's answer is not an additional empirical subject but the
self-shining *sat-cit* that is said to remain when subject–object cognition
ends. Madhva rejects that answer because he takes difference-cognition as a
non-defeasible *pramāṇa* and treats *abheda* statements as similarity or
dependence language. The texts establish the disagreement; they do not, by
themselves, establish Madhva's epistemic premise.

## Finding

There is no textual basis for saying that Madhva simply attacks the crude claim
that a finite person is God. His argument directly denies the final claim made
in Ramaṇa's Sanskrit: that the difference of jīva and Īśvara is not difference
in the reality's nature. Nor is there textual basis for saying that Ramaṇa's
teaching leaves Madhva's final, limited witness intact.

A synthetic use of both can be proposed only by an explicit re-indexing: take
Madhva as describing the irreducible devotional and personal register, while
taking Ramaṇa as denying final objectifiable selfhood. That may be a coherent
modern construction, but it is not Madhva's own conclusion. The non-forced
result of the primary texts is a live philosophical disagreement over whether
the witness itself is finally differentiated.
