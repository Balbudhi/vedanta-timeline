# Does *Vijñāna Vedānta* Require a New Vedānta?

## A textual audit of Ayon Maharaj (Swami Medhananda) on Ramakrishna and Śaṅkara

### Finding

Ramakrishna's *vijñāna* need not be reduced to a conventional Śaṅkara vocabulary, and Medhananda is right to insist on its positive, world-inclusive, devotional force. But the sharp historical contrast on which his proposed “Vijñāna Vedānta” depends is not established. Śaṅkara's final knowledge is neither a merely conceptual recognition of an empty Self nor an escape from allness. His texts repeatedly identify complete knowledge with direct experience, see the one Self in all beings, and say that name and form are true precisely as Sat/Brahman is their Self.

The more exact conclusion is narrower than either caricature. Śaṅkara denies that name-and-form is a second *vastu*, that plurality is co-ultimate with Brahman, or that form delimits Brahman's intrinsic nature. He does **not** deny that what appears has all of its being as Brahman. Ramakrishna's “roof and stairs are of one material” can therefore be read as an existential and devotional articulation of Śaṅkarian non-otherness. It becomes a different ontology only if “Brahman and Śakti are non-different” is made to mean that two co-ultimate realities mutually depend upon one another. The Bengali cited below does not require that inference.

This is a textual argument about the secure commentarial corpus. It does not settle the value of Ramakrishna's distinctive realization-language, nor does it pretend that Śaṅkara wrote Trika, Vallabha, Aurobindo, or Medhananda in advance.

### 1. What Medhananda actually claims

Medhananda's strongest case deserves its full force. In *Infinite Paths to Infinite Reality*, he reconstructs Ramakrishna's *vijñāna* as a fuller realization in which the *nitya* and *līlā*, the impersonal and the personal, and Brahman and Śakti are known together. The *jñānī* reaches the roof by *neti neti*; the *vijñānī* sees that the roof and the stairs are made of the same material. Medhananda does **not** simply call *vijñāna* “higher” than *brahmajñāna*; he carefully calls it greater in the sense of fuller, more intimate, and more comprehensive.

His specific anti-Śaṅkara inference has two steps. First, Śaṅkara allegedly grants final reality only to nirguṇa Brahman, treating Īśvara, Śakti, and the world as unreal limiting appearances. Second, Ramakrishna allegedly gives Brahman and Śakti equal ontological status and mutual ontological dependence. On this reconstruction, the *vijñānī*'s world-inclusive realization cannot be a development internal to Śaṅkarian knowledge; it needs a new Vedānta.

That is a serious argument. It safeguards Ramakrishna from being flattened into a formula of “world is illusion,” takes his Bengali analogies as philosophically consequential, and refuses an Advaita that makes devotion and manifestation expendable. It is also consistent with Medhananda's wider account of religious pluralism: genuine paths disclose real aspects of Infinite Reality and can be salvifically effective, while their historical doctrines may conflict and contain error. He does **not** hold that every metaphysical proposition in every religion is simultaneously true. The relevant distinction is between a genuine disclosure and an unrestricted theory that totalizes it.

The audit below concerns the two disputed inferences: Śaṅkara's alleged “empty” final knowledge, and the move from Ramakrishna's non-difference language to mutual dependence.

### 2. The Bengali: non-difference is not mutual dependence

One recurrent Ramakrishna formulation is:

> **ব্রহ্ম আর শক্তি অভেদ — যিনিই ব্রহ্ম তিনিই শক্তি। যতক্ষণ দেহবুদ্ধি, ততক্ষণ দুটো বলে বোধ হয়। বলতে গেলেই দুটো।**
>
> *brahma ār śakti abheda — yini-i brahma tini-i śakti. yatakṣaṇa dehabuddhi, tatakṣaṇa duṭo bale bodh haya. balte gelei duṭo.*

> “Brahman and Śakti are non-different—the very one who is Brahman is Śakti. So long as body-consciousness remains, they are felt as two; to speak of them, one has to say two.”

The crucial expression is **অভেদ** (*abheda*), the learned Sanskrit formation *a-* + *bheda* (from √*bhid*, “split, divide”): non-difference, absence of division. Bengali **যিনিই … তিনিই** is a relative–correlative construction; the emphatic particle **-ই** makes its force “the very one who … that very one.” It supplies co-reference, not two independent relata.

Ramakrishna can also say, “They are not two separate things; they are one and the same thing” (*duṭā ālāḍā jiniṣ nay; eki jiniṣ*). These formulations plainly permit an identity–modality reading: one non-objectifiable Reality is called Brahman under stillness and Śakti under manifestation. They also explain why language has to speak in two terms. They do not, by grammar alone, entail that Brahman and Śakti are two beings which make each other exist.

This should be described accurately. Medhananda's English “That which is Brahman is also Śakti” is not a demonstrable lexical mistranslation. The issue is the subsequent philosophical escalation:

\[
\textit{abheda} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{conceptual inseparability} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{equal ontological status} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{mutual ontological dependence}.
\]

The first transition is natural; the last is an extra thesis. Dependence normally relates distinguishable items. If the terms are non-different in the strict sense, “mutual dependence” risks reintroducing the two that *abheda* removed. An asymmetrical formulation is cleaner: no manifestation is outside Brahman; Brahman is not bounded, completed, or made necessary by any particular manifestation.

The Bengali needs an edition-controlled collation before anyone alleges a translation error. The present wording is checked against a public *Kathāmṛta* transcription, while the project currently lacks a searchable authoritative five-volume Bengali base. That limitation is why the claim here is **interpretive overreach**, not “mistranslation.”

### 3. Śaṅkara: what the Sanskrit says, and what it does not say

The relevant distinctions are visible in a small number of passages. Pāṇinian analysis here fixes morphology and syntactic scope; it cannot, by itself, decide a commentator's full *tātparya*. Immediate context does that further work.

| Locus | Literal grammatical result | Strict entailment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chāndogya 6.1.4 | *vācā* is instrumental “in/by speech”; *vikāraḥ* is “modification”; *mṛttikā iti eva satyam* restricts final reality to clay. | A modification is not a second independent thing. |
| Chāndogya Bhāṣya 6.3.2 | *sadātmanā* is instrumental singular: “with Sat as its Self”; *sato ’nyatvena* means “in the status of being other than Sat.” | Names/forms are true as Sat-selfhood, false as alleged otherness. |
| Brahma-Sūtra Bhāṣya 2.1.14 | *ananyatva* is `an-anya-tva`, “not-otherness”; Śaṅkara glosses it as the effect's absence apart from its cause. | Dependence is asymmetric, not predicate-for-predicate identity. |
| Gītā Bhāṣya 7.2; 9.1 | *sa-vijñānam*, *svānubhava-yuktam*, and *jñānam vijñāna-sahitam* identify knowledge with direct experience. | Śaṅkarian knowledge is not a merely abstract or empty state. |

#### Chāndogya: the user's central formula is textually real

The Upaniṣadic maxim is:

> *vācārambhaṇaṃ vikāro nāmadheyaṃ mṛttikety eva satyam*
> “A modification is speech-supported, a designation; ‘clay’ alone is real.”

Śaṅkara makes the constraint explicit: *na vikāro nāma vastv asti paramārthataḥ*—“no modification as such exists as a thing in the ultimate sense.” This does not say that no pot appears, functions, is named, or is used. It says pothood has not introduced another *vastu* alongside clay.

The later commentary gives the decisive qualification:

> *sarvaṃ ca nāmarūpādi sadātmanaiva satyaṃ vikārajātaṃ svatas tv anṛtam eva.*
> “All name-and-form and so on is true precisely with Sat as its Self; the whole class of modifications, however, is untrue in its own right.”

> *ataḥ sadātmanā sarvavyavahārāṇāṃ sarvavikārāṇāṃ ca satyatvaṃ, sato ’nyatvenānṛtatvam.*
> “Therefore all transactions and all modifications have truth with Sat as their Self, and falsity in the condition of being other than Sat.”

This is the best textual basis for the formulation at issue: names and forms are not false because Brahman is elsewhere and they are elsewhere; they are false as supposed otherness. Yet *svatas tu anṛtam eva* (“untrue in its own right”) prevents a further conclusion: name and form, **as name and form**, are not thereby made independent or co-ultimate realities. The language is de-reifying, not a new reification of the world.

#### Brahma-Sūtra: Śaṅkara's hard limit must not be evaded

At 2.1.14 Śaṅkara offers the exact definition of non-otherness:

> *kāraṇāt paramārthato ’nanyatvaṃ vyatirekeṇābhāvaḥ kāryasya.*
> “Its ultimate not-otherness from the cause is the effect's absence apart from it.”

This supports the anti-independent-secondness thesis. But the same discussion rejects an opponent's ocean-and-waves proposal that unity and plurality are both finally true. Śaṅkara replies *naivaṃ syāt* (“not so”), calls unity alone *pāramārthika*, and describes plurality as *mithyājñāna-vijṛmbhita*—the expansion of false cognition. At 2.1.27 he calls form-difference *avidyā-kalpita* and says that, in ultimate nature, Brahman remains beyond all transaction and unchanging.

No honest Śaṅkarian reading may soften these denials into mere provisional rhetoric. Nor do they prove that the realized person encounters a blank. Gītā 6.29–31 has the yogin see the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self; Śaṅkara reads the vision as universal Selfhood and the oneness of Vāsudeva and the knower. In 5.8–9 and 3.20, bodily action can continue without the cognition “I act,” including action for *loka-saṅgraha*. The negation is of independent agent, object, and transformation—not of the appearing field's entire being as Brahman.

### 4. *Vijñāna* is already a Śaṅkarian word for completed direct knowledge

This point is often lost when “knowledge” is heard in a modern, merely propositional sense. At Gītā 7.2 Śaṅkara glosses *sa-vijñānam* as knowledge joined to one's own experience (*svānubhava-yuktam*). At 9.1 he similarly calls it knowledge joined with experience (*jñānam vijñāna-sahitam anubhava-yuktam*) and identifies its completed content with *Vāsudevaḥ sarvam*, *ātmaivedaṃ sarvam*, and *ekam evādvitīyam*. *Upadeśasāhasrī* calls this knowledge exceedingly pure and says no gain exceeds the acquisition of one's own nature.

That does not prove that Ramakrishna's technical *vijñānī*, *bhāva-mukha*, or *īśvara-koṭi* is a term Śaṅkara himself uses. It does defeat the weaker contrast: Śaṅkara's *jñānī* has only an impersonal abstraction, whereas Ramakrishna alone discovers the allness of the Real. The textual evidence permits a more economical reading. Ramakrishna's sequential roof-and-stairs teaching can describe the maturation, breadth, embodiment, and devotional expression of an already complete nondual realization; it does not by itself prove a second ontology after it.

The Handbook makes this point unexpectedly well because its chapter “Contemplating Nonduality” is by **Neil Dalal**, not by Medhananda. Dalal describes Śaṅkarian contemplation as exclusive of all identities **as independent entities** and inclusive of them as non-separate from Self/Brahman. That is a specialized reconstruction of Śaṅkara's practice of contemplation. Medhananda's own brief Introduction and his Ramakrishna-focused argument have a different task: to draw a sharp enough ideal-type contrast for a distinctive *Vijñāna Vedānta*. Editorial authorship does not make the two accounts identical, and the difference need not imply ignorance or bad faith.

### 5. Other Vedāntas: real disclosures, real disagreements

The right pluralism is not a device for erasing disagreement. It says that mystical realization, devotion, ethical seriousness, and discernment can disclose genuine dimensions of the divine without licensing every school to make its own grammar exhaustive.

Rāmānuja's *Vedārthasaṅgraha* accepts Brahman as essentially unchanging while construing selves and matter as Brahman's body/modes. Jīva Gosvāmī calls Īśvara the independent possessor of power and jīvas dependent powers; his *acintya-bhedābheda* is not mutual dependence. Vallabha's *avikṛta-pariṇāma* offers an important logical control: a real divine manifestation and divine non-modification need not be contradictory. These systems demonstrate possibilities that a flat “world is simply unreal” slogan misses. They do not make Śaṅkara say, without qualification, that differentiation is an intrinsic and co-ultimate predicate of Brahman.

Madhva is the necessary hard case. He does not merely correct an egoic misuse of “I am Brahman.” His *Anuvyākhyāna* calls the fivefold difference real and beginningless, says it does not cease in liberation, and reads Gītā allness-texts as the one Lord's presence in all distinct beings. He knows the non-difference texts and explains them as similarity, not identity. A constructive nondual synthesis may learn from Madhva's defense of dependence, devotion, ethical seriousness, and the danger of premature spiritual possession. It may not claim that Madhva's final doctrine is covert Advaita. The disagreement is explicit.

The same discipline governs modern comparisons. Aurobindo really says that Śaṅkara was “not an undeveloped mind” but taught undeveloped minds under a Karmakāṇḍa-heavy historical condition. That makes pedagogical selectivity a serious hypothesis. Aurobindo nevertheless criticizes Śaṅkara's system as a one-sided, extreme illusionism; he is not evidence that Śaṅkara secretly taught Integral Vedānta. Likewise, Deleuze's rhizome can be a useful analogy against a single, centralized story of traditions; it cannot prove that incompatible metaphysical propositions are all true. The constructive synthesis must be named as such rather than projected back onto every source.

### Conclusion

Medhananda's major contribution remains: Ramakrishna's *vijñāna* is philosophically serious, its pluralism is not lazy relativism, and manifestation, devotion, and the world's religious plurality require better treatment than a caricature of Advaita permits. But his opposition of Ramakrishna to Śaṅkara is too strong in two places.

First, Śaṅkara's own grammar and commentary say that names and forms are true with Sat as their Self and false only as alleged otherness; completed knowledge is direct, all-inclusive recognition, not sterile self-absorption. Second, Ramakrishna's *abheda* language says non-difference and the inadequacy of two-term speech; it does not itself say that Brahman and Śakti are mutually dependent co-ultimate entities.

No new Vedānta is needed as a metaphysical repair for an allegedly world-empty Śaṅkara. “Vijñāna Vedānta” remains defensible as the name for Ramakrishna's distinctive pedagogy, devotional phenomenology, and modern manifestationist pluralism. The more cautious historical verdict is this: Ramakrishna's positive language can deepen and correct the reception of Śaṅkara without overturning Śaṅkara's central claim. Being does not become a second being; nothing that becomes has being outside Being.
