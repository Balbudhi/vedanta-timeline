# Does *Vijñāna Vedānta* Require a New Vedānta?

## Identity, power, and the alleged dependence of Brahman and Śakti

### Executive finding

The dispute is not settled by calling Ramakrishna either a conventional Śaṅkarin or a simple anti-Śaṅkarin. Nor is it a demonstrable Bengali mistranslation. Ayon Maharaj (Swami Medhananda) deliberately reads Ramakrishna's *abheda* as **inseparability**, not numerical identity, and builds a serious contrast between Śaṅkara's alleged hierarchy and Ramakrishna's world-inclusive *vijñāna*. The pressure point is philosophical: his inference from inseparability to “mutual ontological dependence.”

That inference is invalid if Brahman and Śakti are two names, aspects, or modalities of one nondual Reality. Equality of reality under that description is unobjectionable: neither is an illusory second substance outside the Real. But it does not make one Reality dependent on itself in the way two distinct beings can be mutually dependent. At most, it gives a claim of essential or conceptual co-implication: one cannot adequately conceive the manifesting Reality while abstracting from its power. Calling that **mutual ontological dependence** either mistakes an identity relation for a relation between two entities or uses “dependence” in a very weak, nonstandard sense.

This matters because the best Kashmir Śaiva and Gauḍīya controls make exactly the distinction Medhananda blurs. They affirm an inseparable, even intrinsic, Śakti; they do not thereby posit two co-grounding ultimates. Trika says that free consciousness manifests the universe upon **its own** screen, without another will or material. Jīva Gosvāmī calls Īśvara, the possessor of power, *svatantra* and the powers *asvatantra*. Neither is an Advaitin, and each has real disagreements with Śaṅkara. Both show why “inseparable” need not mean “two co-dependent beings.”

The historical verdict on Śaṅkara is also more exact than Medhananda's contrast permits. Śaṅkara denies that modification, name, and form are an independently real second *vastu*. He does not deny that name and form are true as Sat/Brahman is their Self, or that completed knowledge is direct, all-inclusive recognition. Ramakrishna's “Brahman has become everything” can therefore function as a powerful devotional and phenomenological clarification of Advaitic non-otherness. It becomes a distinct metaphysical doctrine only where it positively attributes intrinsically real differentiated manifestation to the Absolute—precisely the point at which Trika, Gauḍīya Vedānta, Vallabha, and Aurobindo make claims that Śaṅkara's secure commentaries do not make.

## 1. First correction: translation, interpretation, and doctrine are different questions

An earlier draft of this article spoke too loosely of “mistranslation.” That claim is withdrawn. There are three questions, and they must not be merged.

| Question | What the evidence presently supports | What it does **not** support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bengali wording | *abheda* means non-difference; Bengali *jini-i … tini-i* has emphatic co-reference: “the very one who is … is that very one.” | A claim that Medhananda reverses the Bengali's lexical content. |
| Translation choice | “That which is Brahman is also Śakti” is looser than “the very one who is Brahman is Śakti,” but is not plainly false English. | A verdict of fraud or elementary grammatical incompetence. |
| Metaphysical inference | Ramakrishna's images support inseparability and may support an intrinsic-power reading. | The conclusion, without further argument, that two co-ultimate relata mutually ground each other's being. |

The distinction has documentary importance. In the Handbook chapter on Ramakrishna, Jeffery Long notes that Nikhilananda translated *abheda* as “identical,” then proposes “inseparable” as the better rendering and thanks Ayon Maharaj for the suggestion. Medhananda therefore cannot fairly be charged with accidentally translating *abheda* as a relation between two independent substances. He is making a philosophical choice: fire is not identical to “its power to burn,” but fire and its power are inseparable. That choice is intelligible. The question is what follows from it.

The project currently has only a scan of the Bengali *Kathāmṛta* volume 1 and a scan of *Śrī Śrī Māyer Kathā*, not a searchable edition-controlled Bengali corpus. Searchable institutional/public transcriptions of the relevant *Kathāmṛta* entries can be used provisionally, but every final critical quotation should still be collated against a page-identified Udbodhan edition. The scholarly claim must remain an interpretive audit, not an accusation that a Bengali word was mistranslated. The English citations used by Medhananda should be retained and tested; they should not be replaced by invented Bengali certainty.

## 2. The dependence inference: where it succeeds, where it fails

Medhananda's own argument in *Infinite Paths* is explicit. Ramakrishna says Brahman and Śakti are inseparable, like fire and burning power, water and wetness, sun and rays, or gem and radiance. Medhananda then argues that these analogies are bidirectional: accepting fire entails accepting its power, and accepting the power entails accepting fire. He concludes that Brahman and Śakti have equal ontological status and, in contrast with Advaita, “mutual ontological dependence.”

The first three claims must be distinguished.

| Relation | Formal shape | Example | Does it entail two co-dependent beings? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Numerical identity | `B = Ś` | the very one called Brahman is called Śakti | No; there is one referent. |
| Identity with a mode or power | `Ś = power/self-manifestation of B` | fire and its burning capacity | No; a power is not a second bearer. |
| Essential co-implication | `□(B → Ś)` and perhaps `□(Ś → B)` | a genuine fire is not conceived as wholly without burning capacity | No; this may be a truth about the essence or concept of one thing. |
| Ontological grounding | `Ground(B, Ś)` | a property is grounded in its bearer | Ordinarily asymmetric. |
| Mutual ontological dependence | `Ground(B, Ś)` and `Ground(Ś, B)` | two entities cannot be without each other | Only if B and Ś are distinct relata, and then the grounding cycle needs an independent account. |

Thus “equal ontological status” need not itself be an error. If it means, “Śakti is not an illusory object outside Brahman; it is the Real itself under its dynamic aspect,” it agrees with an identity–modality account. The phrase is misleading only if it reintroduces two items at the same ontological level.

The word **therefore** is the problem. Inseparability can establish an essential relation. It does not establish that Brahman needs an entity called Śakti in order to be, or that Śakti needs a distinct entity called Brahman in order to be. If the relation is really non-difference, dependence has been misdescribed: it is the one Reality's non-separation from its own power. If there are genuinely two entities, then a claim of mutual dependence needs an account of what grounds the whole pair and why the pair does not violate the intended nonduality.

The analogies cannot do that work by themselves. Water/wetness and fire/burning are analogies of an entity and an inseparable capacity or characteristic; gem/radiance is an analogy of a bearer and its manifestation. Physical fire may fail to burn in a particular circumstance, and a gem's visible radiance depends on conditions, so these are not rigorous scientific premises. Their religious force is instead anti-separation: do not posit a bare, inactive Brahman over here and a second power over there. That force is fully preserved by identity–modality language.

## 3. Kashmir Śaivism is the decisive comparative control

The strongest reply to a dead or merely illuminative Absolute is not a theory of two balanced entities. It is Trika's account of a single free consciousness whose self-awareness and power are intrinsic. Kṣemarāja's *Pratyabhijñāhṛdayam* begins:

> *citiḥ svatantrā viśvasiddhihetuḥ*
> “Consciousness, free/independent, is the cause of the manifestation of the universe.”

His commentary is even clearer. The supreme consciousness is *paraśaktirūpā* (“of the form of supreme power”) and *śivabhaṭṭārakābhinnā* (“non-different from Śiva”). When an objection asks how cause-and-effect language can apply if the universe is not different from consciousness, Kṣemarāja answers: consciousness itself, in its clear free nature, appears as the innumerable universes. That, he says, is the whole truth of “cause” and “effect.”

The next aphorism removes the possibility of external co-dependence:

> *svecchayā svabhittau viśvam unmīlayati*
> “By its own will it unfolds the universe upon its own screen.”

Kṣemarāja explicitly glosses *svecchayā* as “not through another's will” and *svabhittau* as “not anywhere else”; consciousness needs no external material basis. This is not a second Śakti propping up an otherwise incomplete Śiva. It is Śiva/Consciousness's own freedom, reflective awareness, and power. The tradition does distinguish itself from what it calls *brahmavāda*: it makes *vimarśa* or Śakti intrinsic to consciousness rather than treating power only as empirical. That is a real difference from Śaṅkara. But it is not Medhananda's mutual-dependence model.

Trika therefore both supports and limits the proposed synthesis.

- It supports the claim that manifestation can be non-different from the Absolute and that realization can recognize the world as consciousness's own appearing rather than a second reality.
- It supports the claim that Śakti is not sheer nothingness or an external material.
- It does **not** erase its disagreement with Śaṅkara's explicit denial that action and form-difference intrinsically characterize Brahman.
- It does **not** treat Śiva and Śakti as two substances mutually giving one another being.

The Sanskrit itself is the key: *svatantrā*, “self-governing/independent,” is the opposite of a dependence claim. “Śiva without Śakti is powerless” in later Śākta rhetoric is most defensibly read as rejecting the abstraction of an inert Śiva, not as positing a second entity on which Śiva literally relies. If a reader instead makes it literal mutual dependence, the reader owes a nondual account of the two relata.

## 4. The Vedāntic analogies already have technical homes

The fire/radiance/sunlight imagery is not evidence for a single conclusion. Different Vedāntas use it with different metaphysical machinery.

**Gauḍīya Vedānta.** Jīva Gosvāmī uses the fire-and-radiance analogy to establish the Lord's *svābhāvika śakti*: power is innate, proceeds without external effort, and is inseparable from its possessor. Yet his terminology preserves asymmetry: “Īśvara, the possessor of power, is independent; the jīvas and so on, his powers, are not independent” (*īśvaraḥ śaktimān svatantraḥ; jīvādayas tu tacchaktayo ’svatantrāḥ*). This is not Śaṅkara: Jīva affirms *acintya-bhedābheda*, a real difference-and-non-difference, and ranks Bhagavān above an impersonal Brahman realization. But it refutes the thought that inseparable power must make the possessor mutually dependent on the power.

**Viśiṣṭādvaita.** Rāmānuja's *apṛthaksiddhi* (“non-separate establishment”) and body–self model give a second control. Selves and matter cannot be established separately from Brahman as their inner Self; this does not make Brahman and world symmetric co-grounding substances. The dependence runs from body/mode to Self, even as the qualified whole cannot be described without its modes.

**Śuddhādvaita.** Vallabha's *avikṛta-pariṇāma* is the crucial logical control. It aims to affirm real divine manifestation while denying that Brahman loses or becomes other than itself. This demonstrates that a positive account of manifestation and divine immutability are not contradictory. It does not prove that Śaṅkara himself taught real *pariṇāma*; it shows that the debate concerns the status of manifestation, not a simple choice between blank Being and worldly change.

These comparisons make the philosophical point more exact. “Ground of being” and “non-different manifestation” are not rivals. A manifestation can have no being outside its ground without being an external second. The open question is whether differentiation is merely sublatable appearance in the final account, or an eternally/intrinsically real articulation of the Absolute. That is where the classical schools disagree.

## 5. Ramakrishna and Vivekananda: the corpus is internally richer than Medhananda's contrast

Ramakrishna's reported language strongly supports the positive side that Medhananda protects. He repeatedly speaks of divine consciousness becoming all, of the Mother as all, and of the *vijñānī* who sees the very Reality reached by negation as the universe and living beings. In Medhananda's own citation of the Kālī vision, the image, altar, vessels, threshold, floor, cat, and even a wicked person are seen as consciousness or the Mother's Śakti. This is not a sterile escape from appearance.

The Bengali makes the identity–modality reading explicit. In the 22 September 1883 entry, Ramakrishna says:

> **যিনিই ব্রহ্ম তিনিই শক্তি। … যখন সৃষ্টি, স্থিতি, প্রলয় করেন তখন তাঁকে শক্তি বলে। দুটা আলাদা জিনিস নয়। একই জিনিস।**

> “The very one who is Brahman is Śakti. … When [that One] creates, sustains, and dissolves, [that One] is called Śakti. They are not two separate things; they are one and the same thing.”

The relative–correlative **যিনিই … তিনিই** (*jini-i … tini-i*) and the emphatic **-ই** identify one referent; **একই জিনিস** means “the same thing.” “When [the One] creates … [the One] is called Śakti” is exactly a difference of designation according to activity. It does not say that one entity needs another entity in order to exist. In the same passage Ramakrishna adds that all that is seen and thought—the world, meditation, meditator, devotion, and love—is the glory of *Ādyāśakti/ciṭśakti*.

Medhananda's hardest textual evidence must also be kept. In the 19 August 1883 entry, Ramakrishna reports a Vedāntic formulation: creation, dissolution, living beings, and world are Śakti's play; on inquiry they are dream-like, Brahman alone is the reality, and Śakti too is dream-like. He then says that, short of samādhi, one cannot get beyond Śakti's domain and concludes: “Brahman and Śakti are *abheda*; accepting one requires accepting the other,” followed by the fire/power, sun/rays, milk/whiteness analogies. This is a genuine corrective to a dismissive treatment of Śakti. It does not settle whether the correction is asymmetric non-otherness, inseparable aspecthood, or two co-ultimate aspects; that is the philosophical issue under dispute.

But the same Ramakrishna material also resists a simple two-aspect ontology. In the *Kathāmṛta* passage cited in the Handbook, he says that Brahman and Primal Energy first appear to be two, but after Brahman-knowledge there is no differentiation: One without a second. That supports the claim that two-term language belongs to a level of presentation or practice; it cannot simply be used to infer two ultimate components. Ramakrishna's force lies in holding the inward movement of *neti neti* and the outward recognition of divinity together, not necessarily in revising the One into a partnership.

Vivekananda supplies particularly useful controls because he speaks both in uncompromising Advaitic and Śākta idioms.

- In “The Women of India” he calls the Absolute the background of the universe and says that its powers are spoken of as “She”: “This Śakti of the Brahman” is the personal divine language. The grammar is possessive and modal—Brahman's Śakti—not a second ground.
- In his Harvard lecture, he says the personal God remains “on a better basis” through the Impersonal, then states the asymmetry plainly: “without the Impersonal, the Personal cannot remain.” He does not say the converse. He calls the Personal God true insofar as it is that very Impersonal, yet not absolute truth as a separately delimited object.
- In his account of *jīvanmukti*, the phenomenal image can continue for one whose organs continue, but it no longer deceives; the prison of misery is recognized as Sat–Cit–Ānanda. This is extraordinarily close to the non-reification reading of Śaṅkara.
- Elsewhere he can call the world dreamlike, urge renunciation, insist that the Absolute is beyond personal predicates, and say that the same universe will appear as God/Absolute when the body-identification dream falls away. These are not embarrassing counterexamples. They show why extracting a single “Vivekananda ontology” without audience, genre, and purpose produces distortion.

The same caution applies to Sarada Devi. Her Bengali *Śrī Śrī Māyer Kathā* is already in the source tree as a 434-page scan, but its language has not yet been transcribed and checked against a controlled edition. This article therefore does not put English paraphrases of her “dream” teachings into evidence. That is a research task, not a license to project a desired conclusion onto her.

## 6. What Śaṅkara says: a completed knowledge, but an asymmetric ontology

The strongest reading of Śaṅkara has to keep two series of statements together.

**The positive series.** At *Chāndogya Bhāṣya* 6.3.2, Śaṅkara says:

> *sarvaṃ ca nāmarūpādi sadātmanaiva satyaṃ vikārajātaṃ svatas tv anṛtam eva.*
> “All name and form and so on is true precisely with Sat as its Self; the whole class of modifications is untrue in its own right.”

His own summary is even more exact: transactions and modifications have truth *sadātmanā*, “with Sat as their Self,” and falsity *sato ’nyatvena*, “in the status of being other than Sat.” The grammar supports the claim that falsity is alleged otherness, not the absence of all appearance or all Brahman-being.

His *Gītā* commentary likewise refuses to reduce knowledge to a concept. He glosses *sa-vijñānam* (7.2) as knowledge joined to one's own direct experience, *svānubhava-yuktam*. At 9.1 he calls it knowledge joined with realization and gives its complete content as *Vāsudevaḥ sarvam*, *ātmaivedaṃ sarvam*, and *ekam evādvitīyam*. The knower sees the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self, and can act without the cognition “I act.” These are not descriptions of an empty phenomenological blank.

**The limiting series.** The same Śaṅkara says at *Chāndogya* 6.1.4 and *Brahma-Sūtra Bhāṣya* 2.1.14 that modification is “speech-supported,” a designation, and that no modification exists as a *vastu* in the ultimate sense. He defines the effect's *ananyatva* as its absence apart from the cause. And he rejects the opponent who says unity and plurality are equally real like ocean and waves: unity alone is ultimate; plurality is the expansion of false cognition. At 2.1.27 form-difference is *avidyā-kalpita*, constructed/imputed through ignorance; Brahman in its ultimate nature is beyond all transaction and unchanging.

The conclusion is asymmetrical:

> The world is true as Brahman/Sat is its Self; it is not true as an independently established modification or a co-ultimate predicate that delimits Brahman.

This supports a Ramakrishnaan–Śaṅkarian synthesis up to a point: realization removes independent secondness, not visual content; devotion and action can continue without doership; divinity is recognized in all. It does not establish that Śaṅkara grants manifestation, as differentiated manifestation, the same final status given to it in Trika, Vallabha, or Aurobindo.

## 7. Does *vijñāna* require a “new Vedānta”?

Three answers must be separated.

1. **As a new spiritual pedagogy and phenomenology:** yes, the label is defensible. Ramakrishna's roof-and-stairs, *bhāva-mukha*, Mother language, and all-paths practice form a distinctive modern articulation. Medhananda's achievement is to show that this is philosophy, not merely devotional ornament.

2. **As a refutation of a world-empty caricature of Śaṅkara:** no. Śaṅkara's texts already contain direct experience, universal Self-vision, action without doership, and the truth of names/forms as Sat-selfhood. Medhananda's recurrent formula—“the Advaitic *jñānī* dismisses Śakti and the world as unreal”—is too blunt unless “unreal” is immediately specified as non-independent and sublatable-as-other.

3. **As a doctrine that differentiated manifestation is intrinsically and positively real in the Absolute:** it is no longer simply Śaṅkara's secure *bhāṣya* doctrine. It resembles Trika, Vallabha, Gauḍīya Vedānta, and Aurobindo in different ways. A constructive synthesis can affirm that these are genuine disclosures without claiming that their final global claims are all historically identical.

Medhananda is therefore strongest when he treats Ramakrishna as a manifestationist, pluralist, and world-inclusive religious philosopher. He is weakest when he converts a real difference of emphasis and a debated ontology of Śakti into a simple opposition: empty Śaṅkara versus full Ramakrishna. Conversely, an Advaitic synthesis is strongest when it says that no form has being outside Brahman and that nondual realization can embrace forms without being delimited by them. It is weakest when it calls Śaṅkara's explicit denials of real modification and co-ultimate plurality merely provisional pedagogy.
