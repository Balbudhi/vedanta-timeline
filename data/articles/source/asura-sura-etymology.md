# *Asura* and *Sura*: Tracing a Popular Etymology to Its Sources

*An investigation into Swami Chinmayananda's gloss that* sura *is cognate with* sur *("harmony") and that* asura *therefore means "the inharmonious" — checked against the Uṇādi-sūtras, the Aṣṭādhyāyī and its commentaries, the nirvacana tradition, Śaṅkara's bhāṣyas, and the Ṛgvedic record, all on disk in this project and in* `Balbudhi/prakriya`.

---

## The claim under examination

The gloss circulates widely in modern Vedānta teaching: *sura* is connected with *sur* — tone, harmony, being in tune — and *a-sura* is therefore the negation, the one who is out of tune, discordant, inharmonious. It is attributed here to Swami Chinmayananda.

Three separate questions have to be kept apart, because the answers differ:

1. **Is this the derivation the Sanskrit grammatical tradition actually gives for *sura* and *asura*?** No. The tradition derives them from entirely different roots, and derives them *independently of each other*.
2. **Is there anything in the tradition that could have generated the gloss?** Yes — three things, none of them an etymology of *sura*, all of them close enough to be the seed.
3. **What is the historically correct account?** *Asura* is the primitive; *sura* is a late back-formation from it. This is not a modern Indological invention — Monier-Williams states it in the dictionary entry, and the Ṛgvedic evidence on disk confirms it decisively.

What follows works through each.

---

## I. What the grammarians actually derive

### I.1 *Asura* — Uṇādi 1.42, *aser uran*

The Pāṇinian system does not derive *asura* by regular kṛt-affixation. It is handled in the Uṇādi-sūtras, the supplementary list of irregular primary derivatives that Pāṇini licenses wholesale at A 3.3.1 (*uṇādayo bahulam*). The relevant sūtra, with the Siddhānta-Kaumudī's gloss:

> **असेरुरन् ।** असुरः । प्रज्ञाद्यण् । आसुरः ।
>
> *aser uran.* — "*asuraḥ*. [Then] the *aṇ* of the *prajñādi* class: *āsuraḥ*."
>
> — Uṇādi-sūtra 1.42 (pañcapādī), with Siddhānta-Kaumudī serial उ42
> (`Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/unadi/unadisutra_ashtadhyayi_com_pancapadi.json`, entry `1042`;
> `data/recension_mirror/siddhantakaumudi/per_sutra/sk_उ42.json`)

The analysis is: root **अस्** + Uṇādi affix **उरन्** (of which *n* is an *it*-marker, leaving *ura*) → **असुर**. The neighbouring sūtras confirm the reading: 1.43 *maseś ca* forms *masura* from √*mas*, and 1.44 *śāvaśer āptau* forms *śvaśura*. So *asura* is morphologically a sibling of *śvaśura* ("father-in-law"), not of anything containing a privative *a-*.

Which √*as*? The tradition is not unanimous. Two answers are on disk:

- **√अस् (2), "to throw, cast"** — Monier-Williams reads the Uṇādi sūtra this way: `a/sura mfn. (√ 2. as, Uṇ.), spiritual, incorporeal, divine, RV` (`Balbudhi/prakriya: src/prakriya/data/lexicon/mw.json`, s.v. `asura`).
- **√असु, "to throw" (*asu kṣepaṇe*) with the affix कुरच्** — Haradatta's *Padamañjarī* on A 2.4.61, glossing the *taulvalyādi* gaṇa (which contains the patronymic **आसुरि**): `ठसु क्षेपणेऽ, कुरच् - असुरः` (`Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/padamanjari/padamanjari_ashtadhyayi_com.json`, entry `24061`). The opening character is a mangled quotation mark in the source; read *'asu kṣepaṇe', kurac — asuraḥ*.

Apte records a third, semantically richer route in the same bracket: **असु = प्राण**, the vital breath, with *-ra* as a *matvartha* (possessive) suffix:

> `asura a. [asu-ra Uṇ. 1.42] … or from asu, asuḥ prāṇaḥ, tena tadvanto bhavanti, ro matvarthe`
>
> — "or from *asu*: *asu* is the vital breath; by it they are possessed of it; *-ra* in the possessive sense."
> (`Balbudhi/prakriya: src/prakriya/data/lexicon/apte.json`, s.v. `asura`)

On this reading *asura* means simply **"the one that has life-breath"** — which is exactly what the Ṛgvedic usage requires (§III), and exactly what the adjectival sense in MW records: "spiritual, incorporeal, divine."

### I.2 *Sura* — Uṇādi 2.25, a different root entirely

The tradition does *not* derive *sura* from *asura*, and does not derive *asura* from *sura*. When it needs a derivation for *sura*, it reaches for a completely unrelated root:

> **सुसूधागृधिभ्यः क्रन् ।** सुरः । सूरः । धीरः । गृध्रः ।
>
> *su-sū-dhā-gṛdhibhyaḥ kran.* — "*suraḥ. sūraḥ. dhīraḥ. gṛdhraḥ.*"
>
> — Uṇādi-sūtra 2.25 (pañcapādī), Siddhānta-Kaumudī serial उ182
> (`Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/unadi/...json`, entry `2025`; `.../per_sutra/sk_उ182.json`)

Root **सु/सू** + affix **क्रन्** (*k* and *n* are *it*-markers; the affix is *-ra*) → **सुर**, alongside **सूर** (the sun) and, in the next sūtra, **शूर** (hero). Apte glosses the semantic content of the root at *sūra*:

> `sūraḥ [suvati prerayati karmaṇi lokān udayena, sū-kran, Uṇ. 2. 24]`
>
> — "*sūra*: he who impels (*suvati prerayati*) the world to action by his rising."
> (Apte, s.v. `sUraH`; note Apte's numbering runs one behind the recension on disk)

So on the Uṇādi route, *sura* is "the impeller / the one who brings forth" — the same root family as *sūrya*, *savitṛ*, *prasava*. Apte, however, prefers a different analysis for *sura* specifically:

> `suraḥ [suṣṭhu rāti dadāty abhīṣṭam su-rā-ka]`
>
> — "*sura*: one who **gives well** (*suṣṭhu rāti*), who bestows what is desired": *su-* + √*rā* ("to give") + *ka*.
> (Apte, s.v. `suraH`)

**Neither analysis has anything to do with tone, harmony, or *svara*.** And neither treats *a-* in *asura* as a negation.

### I.3 Pāṇini's own evidence: *asura* is a stem, *sura* is not in the grammar

This is the most telling datum in the whole file. A scan of all 3,951 sūtras of the Aṣṭādhyāyī (`Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/gretil/sa_pANini-aSTAdhyAyI.txt`) returns exactly two hits on the string:

- **A 4.4.123 — असुरस्य स्वम्** (*asurasya svam*) — "[the affix *yat*] after *asura* in the sense 'belonging to the asura'." The Kāśikā's example is Vedic, accented: *asuryàṃ vā etat pātraṃ yat kulālakṛtam cakravṛttam* (Maitrāyaṇī Saṃhitā 1.8.3) — "asuric indeed is that vessel which is potter-made, turned on a wheel."
- **A 5.4.135 — गन्धस्येदुत्पूतिसुसुरभिभ्यः** — here *surabhi*, not *sura*.

Plus **A 4.4.124 मायायामण्** (*māyāyām aṇ*), immediately following, which gives *āsurī* specifically in the sense of *māyā*: Kāśikā cites *āsurī māyā svadhayā kṛtāsi* (Vājasaneyi-Saṃhitā 11.69).
(All from `Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/kashika_offline.json`.)

Pāṇini therefore composed **a dedicated sūtra pair for *asura*** — and **never mentions *sura* at all**. A word that needs its own two sūtras is a word the grammarian regards as basic and high-frequency. A word absent from the entire grammar is one that, in Pāṇini's language, was not yet doing significant work.

Two further Pāṇinian data are worth recording:

**(a) *Asura* is a warrior-clan name.** At **A 5.3.117 पर्श्वादियौधेयादिभ्यामणञौ**, the Kāśikā's *parśvādi* gaṇa — a list of *āyudhajīvi-saṅgha*, "confederations that live by arms" — reads: *parśu. **asura**. rakṣas. bāhlīka. vayas. marut. daśārha. piśāca. viśāla. aśani. kārṣāpaṇa. satvat. vasu.* Examples: *āsuraḥ, āsurau, asurāḥ*. MW records the same: `a/sura m. pl. N. of a warrior-tribe (g. parśv-ādi)`. In the grammarians' world, *asura* names a **people**, sitting in a list with *marut*, *satvat*, and *bāhlīka* — not a metaphysical antonym.

**(b) The word is itself subject to the r/l alternation.** The vārttika on **A 8.2.18 कृपो रो लः** reads: *vāla-mūla-laghv-**asur**-ālam-aṅgulīnāṃ vā ro lam āpadyate* — "optionally *r* becomes *l* in *vāla*, *mūla*, *laghu*, ***asura***, *alam*, *aṅguli*." Hence **असुरः, असुलः**. Hold on to this; it returns in §V.

## II. The nirvacana tradition: what the *interpreters* say

The Uṇādi derivations are formal. The *nirvacana* (semantic-etymological) tradition is where meaning gets assigned, and it is here that the "negation" reading first appears — as **one option among several**, not as the received account.

Apte's *asura* entry preserves the whole cluster. Reading his bracket out in full:

| # | Analysis | Rendering |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `a suratāḥ sthāneṣu na suṣṭhu ratāḥ, sthāneṣu capalāḥ ity arthaḥ` (attributed to **Nir.**) | "*a-surāḥ*: not well-settled (*na suṣṭhu ratāḥ*) in their stations — i.e. unsteady in their places." |
| 2 | `astāḥ pracyāvitā devaiḥ sthānebhyaḥ` | "*asta* — hurled, dislodged from their stations by the gods." (√*as*, "to throw" — the Uṇādi root) |
| 3 | `asuḥ prāṇaḥ, tena tadvanto bhavanti, ro matvarthe` | "*asu* = breath; possessed of it; *-ra* possessive." |
| 4 | `sor devān asṛjata tat surāṇāṃ suratvam, asoḥ asurān asṛjata tad asurāṇām asuratvam; soḥ = praśastād ātmanaḥ pradeśāt` | "From *su* — the excellent region of himself — he emitted the gods: that is the *sura*-hood of the *sura*s. From *asu* he emitted the *asura*s: that is the *asura*-hood of the *asura*s." |

Note what analysis **1** does. It splits *asura* as *a-* + *su-* + *ratāḥ* — "not **well**-settled" — where the *su-* is the ordinary prefix *su* ("well, good"), not the noun *sura*. **This is the closest thing in the classical tradition to the Chinmayananda gloss**, and it is genuinely close: it makes *asura* mean *not-in-a-good-state*, *unsteady*, *capala*. Translate *capala* as "unsettled, restless, out of true," and you are one homiletic step from "inharmonious."

Analysis **4** is the Brāhmaṇa-style creation etiology, and it is the one that requires *sura* and *asura* to be genuine opposites — which is precisely the assumption §III shows to be secondary.

**Provenance caveat:** the *Nirukta* is **not on disk** in either repository. Analysis 1 is quoted here from Apte's citation of it, and the *Nirukta* reference is Apte's, unnumbered. Analysis 4 is likewise given by Apte without a locus. Both should be treated as *reported* until the primary is acquired; see §VII.

## III. The Ṛgvedic record — decisive, and on disk

The Aufrecht/GRETIL Ṛgveda in this repository (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/rigveda_aufrecht_gretil.txt`) settles the historical question.

### III.1 *Asura* is frequent, ancient, and overwhelmingly honorific

In the older layers it is a **title of the highest gods**:

> *kṣáyann asmábhyam **ásura** prácetā rā́jann énāṁsi śiśrathaḥ kṛtā́ni* — RV 1.24.14 (line 838)
> "Ruling for us, O **asura**, O wise one, O king [Varuṇa], loosen the sins we have committed."

> *hiraṇyahasto **ásuraḥ** sunīthaḥ sumṛḷīkaḥ svávām̐ yātv arvā́ṅ* — RV 1.35.10 (line 1296)
> "Let the golden-handed **asura** [Savitṛ], good guide, gracious, self-possessed, come hither."

> *arvā́ṅ eténa stanayitnúnehy apó niṣiñcánn **ásuraḥ** pitā́ naḥ* — RV 5.83.6 (line 13085)
> "Come hither with this thunder, pouring down the waters — **our father, the asura**." (Parjanya)

> *mahát tád vṛ́ṣṇo **ásurasya** nā́mā viśvarūpo amṛ́tāni tasthau* — RV 3.38.4 (line 8517)
> "Great is that name of the bull **asura**; taking all forms, he has attained the immortal states."

The pejorative sense exists but is later and clearly marked — and the marker is instructive:

> *anāyudhā́so **ásurā** **ádevāś** cakréṇa tā́m̐ apa vapa ṛjīṣin* — RV 8.96.9 (line 22760)
> "Those weaponless **asuras**, **godless** — sweep them away with your wheel."

The opposition in the Ṛgveda is ***asura* vs. *deva***. It is never *asura* vs. *sura*.

### III.2 *Asuratva* is a property **of the devas**

The strongest single piece of evidence is the refrain of **RV 3.55**, a hymn of twenty-two verses in which every single verse ends with the same line (verified: 22 occurrences in the file):

> ***mahád devā́nām **ásuratvam** ékam***
> "Great and one is the **asura-hood of the gods**."
> — RV 3.55.1-22, e.g. line 8973: *vratā́ devā́nām úpa nú prabhū́ṣan mahád devā́nām **asuratvám** ékam*

The abstract noun *asuratva* — "asura-nature, asura-power, lordship" — is predicated **of the devas themselves**, twenty-two times, as the hymn's whole point. Beside it stands *asurya* in the same positive sense: *dīrghādhíyo rákṣamāṇā **asuryàm*** (RV 2.27.4), *ná vā́ u yoṣad rudrā́d **asuryàm*** (RV 2.33.9), "nor does **asura-lordship** depart from Rudra."

Whatever *asura* meant in the oldest layer, **it was something the gods had.** A word cannot simultaneously be the negation of "god" and an abstract quality attributed to the gods in a hymn's refrain.

### III.3 *Sura* does not occur in the Ṛgveda at all

A full tokenization of the file (all word-forms beginning *sur-*) yields **zero** occurrences of a bare *sura / suraḥ / surāḥ / surāṇām* in any inflected form. Every *sur-* token in the Ṛgveda is one of:

- a ***su-* compound** of some other word: *su-rūpa*, *su-ratha* (RV 1.22.2, 2.18.5, 3.14.7, 4.2.4, 4.4.8, 5.57.2, 7.36.4, 8.5.10, 8.68.16, 8.74.14, 10.75.8), *su-rādhas*, *su-ruc*, *su-retas*, *su-rabhi*, *su-rukma*, *su-ratna*, *su-rātaya*, *su-rekṇas*, *su-raśmi*, *su-rāśva*;
- *su-raṇa*, "joyous" (RV 3.29.14, *ná ní miṣati **suraṇó** divé-dive*), which MW confirms as *su-rá/ṇa*;
- **surā́**, the *liquor* (RV 1.116.7, *śatáṃ kumbhā́m̐ asiñcataṃ **surā́yāḥ***), a word of quite different sense.

**There is no *sura* meaning "god" anywhere in the Ṛgveda.** The word for god is *deva*.

### III.4 The word arrives late, and thinly

The Gītā, on disk (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/bhagavadgita_mula_bori.txt`), preserves the asymmetry perfectly. *Sura* appears three times, always inside a compound and always as a metrical filler for *deva*: *sura-gaṇāḥ* (10.2), *sura-saṅghāḥ* (11.21), *yakṣa-**asura**-siddha-saṅghāḥ* (11.22). *Asura / āsura*, by contrast, carries the whole doctrinal weight of chapter 16 — *daivo āsura eva ca* (16.6), *āsurīṃ sampadam* (16.4), *janā na vidur āsurāḥ* (16.7) — and appears at 7.15, 9.12, 17.6 besides.

MW draws the conclusion the evidence forces, in the dictionary entries themselves:

> `sura m. (prob. fr. asura as if fr. a-sura, and as sita fr. a-sita; thought by some to be connected with 2. svar) a god, divinity`
>
> `a/sura [In later Sanskṛt sura has been formed from asura, as sita from asita, q.v.]`

**This is the answer to question 3.** *Sura* is a **back-formation**: later speakers re-analysed the *a-* of *asura* as the privative prefix *nañ* and extracted a positive counterpart that had never existed. The parallel MW gives is exact and famous — *asita* means "black," and *sita* "white" was manufactured from it the same way.

So the popular gloss is *inverted*. It treats *sura* as the base and *asura* as its negation. Historically, *asura* is the base, and *sura* is the artefact of reading it as a negation.

## IV. Śaṅkara: the psychological reading, which *is* traditional

Where the popular gloss is on firm ground is in its **substance** — the treatment of *deva* and *asura* as two dispositions inside one human being — even though its **etymology** is not. Śaṅkara supplies the substance directly, and he is on disk.

### IV.1 Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3.1 — Śaṅkara's own nirvacana of *asura*

Commenting on *dvayā ha prājāpatyā devāś cāsurāś ca* ("two-fold were the descendants of Prajāpati: gods and asuras"), Śaṅkara asks flatly *kathaṃ punas teṣāṃ devāsuratvam?* — "how are they *deva* and *asura* in the first place?" — and answers (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/brhadaranyaka_upanisad_gretil.txt`, lines 539-541):

> *śāstra-janita-jñāna-karma-bhāvitā **dyodanāt** devā bhavanti | ta eva svābhāvika-pratyakṣānumāna-janita-dṛṣṭa-prayojana-karma-jñāna-bhāvitā **asurāḥ** | **sveṣv evāsuṣu ramaṇāt**, **surebhyo vā devebhyo 'nyatvāt**.*
>
> "Suffused with the knowledge and action born of scripture, they are *devas*, **from shining** (*dyodana*). Those very same [powers], suffused with the natural, perception- and inference-born knowledge and action aimed at visible ends, are the *asuras* — **because they revel in their own *asus* (vital breaths / sense-life)**, or **because they are other than the *suras*, the *devas***."

Three things here matter enormously.

1. Śaṅkara reads *deva* from √*dyut/dyu*, "to shine" — the standard nirvacana, which he repeats in the Kaṭha bhāṣya: *taṃ devaṃ **dyotanāj** jñānādi-guṇavantam* (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/katha_upanisad_sankara_bhasya_gretil.txt`).
2. His **primary** gloss of *asura* is **`asuṣu ramaṇāt`** — "from revelling in the *asus*." This is the *asu* + possessive route (Apte's analysis 3, §II) turned into a moral psychology: the *asura* is the sense-power that has turned toward its own vital gratification. Śaṅkara reuses the same idea at Gītā 9.3, calling such people *asu-tṛpaḥ*, "gratifiers of the vital breath" (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/shankara_gita_bhasya.txt`, line 6014).
3. His **alternative** gloss is `surebhyo vā devebhyo 'nyatvāt` — "**or** because they are other than the *sura*s." **Śaṅkara does record the a-*sura* reading.** He records it second, as an option, and he does not build anything on it. But it is in the tradition, in the most authoritative Advaitic commentary there is.

The referents, note, are *the prāṇas — the sense-powers themselves* (*tasyaiva prajāpateḥ prāṇā vāgādayaḥ*). The war of devas and asuras is a war between two modes of one person's own faculties.

### IV.2 Chāndogya 1.2.1 — the war is in every body, beginninglessly

> *anyonyābhibhavodbhava-rūpaḥ saṃgrāma iva sarva-prāṇiṣu **pratideham** devāsura-saṅgrāmo **'nādi-kāla-pravṛtta** ity abhiprāyaḥ.*
>
> "The purport is that this deva-asura war, in the form of the mutual overpowering and rising of each, goes on **in every body**, in all living beings, having commenced **from beginningless time**."
> (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/chandogya_upanisad_gretil.txt`, line 251)

### IV.3 Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.2.3 — the strongest statement

Commenting on the *da-da-da* passage (Prajāpati's threefold instruction to gods, humans, and asuras: *dāmyata, datta, dayadhvam*), Śaṅkara offers this alternative reading (`data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/brhadaranyaka_upanisad_gretil.txt`, lines 10341-10343):

> *athavā **na devā asurā vānye kecana vidyante manuṣyebhyaḥ** | manuṣyāṇām evādāntā ye 'nyair uttamair guṇaiḥ saṃpannās te devāḥ, lobha-pradhānā manuṣyāḥ, tathā hiṃsā-parāḥ krūrā asurāḥ | ta eva manuṣyā adāntatvādi-doṣa-trayam apekṣya devādi-śabda-bhājo bhavanti, itarāṃś ca guṇān **sattva-rajas-tamāṃsy** apekṣya.*
>
> "Or rather: **there are no devas or asuras whatever apart from human beings.** Among humans, those who — though undisciplined — are endowed with other excellent qualities, they are the devas; those in whom greed predominates are the humans; and those bent on violence, the cruel, are the asuras. Those very human beings come to bear the words 'deva' and the rest by reference to the three defects beginning with lack of self-control, and by reference to the other qualities, **sattva, rajas, and tamas**."

This is the whole modern pedagogical reading — *devas and asuras are guṇa-indexed dispositions within the human being* — stated by Śaṅkara in the eighth century. Chinmayananda's substantive teaching is orthodox and well-anchored. It is only the *word-derivation* that is not.

### IV.4 Two more loci worth having

- **Īśa Upaniṣad 3**, *asuryā nāma te lokāḥ*, with Śaṅkara: *paramātma-bhāvam advayam apekṣya **devādayo 'py asurāḥ*** — "with reference to the non-dual state of the supreme Self, **even the gods and the rest are asuras**." The category is relative, not a fixed species.
- **Chāndogya 8.8.5**, *asurāṇāṃ hy eṣopaniṣat* — "for this is the *upaniṣad* (secret doctrine) of the asuras": that the body is the self. Śaṅkara cites it at Gītā 9.3 as *asurāṇām upaniṣadaṃ deha-mātrātma-darśanam*. The *āsura* position is thus defined **doctrinally** — materialism — not by temperament alone.
- **Gītā 16.6** where Śaṅkara grounds the entire *daiva/āsura* chapter in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka verse: *dvayā ha vai prājāpatyā devāś cāsurā ca [BAU 1.3.1] iti śruteḥ*. The chain Gītā 16 → BṛhU 1.3.1 → Śaṅkara's *asuṣu ramaṇāt* is explicit in the commentary itself.

## V. So where does "harmony" come from?

No traditional source derives *sura* from *svara*. But three real things sit close enough to have produced the gloss, and one of them is in the dictionary.

### V.1 The Hindi pun — almost certainly the proximate source

Hindi **सुर** *sur*, "musical note, tune, pitch," is the *tadbhava* — the naturally evolved descendant — of Sanskrit **स्वर** *svara*. It is a *different word* from Sanskrit **सुर** *sura*, "god," which happens to be spelled identically in Devanāgarī after the *tadbhava* development. In Hindi, **बेसुरा** *besurā* means precisely "out of tune, discordant."

For a teacher working in a Hindi-speaking milieu the identification is nearly irresistible: *sur* → in tune; *a-sur* → out of tune. The pun is exact in Hindi and non-existent in Sanskrit. Apte keeps the two roots properly apart: *svaraḥ* is `[svar-ac, svṛ-ap vā]` — from √*svṛ*, "to sound" — with the senses "sound, voice, note of the musical scale, vowel, accent." *Sura* is from √*su/sū* or *su-*√*rā*. Different roots, different families.

### V.2 The dictionary itself records a *svar* connection

The gloss is not pure invention. MW's *sura* entry, quoted in full above, contains the parenthesis:

> `(… thought by some to be connected with 2. svar)`

**svar** (MW 4) is "the sun, sunshine, light, lustre; bright space or sky, heaven" — cognate with Greek *hēlios* and Latin *sol* — and **svar** (MW 2) is a verbal root "to shine." A *sura* connected to *svar* would be "a shining one, a heaven-dweller" — semantically identical to *deva* from *dyotana*, and a perfectly respectable proposal.

The slippage that produces "harmony" is between **svar** ("light, heaven") and **svara** ("tone, accent, vowel"). These are formally distinct, but the tradition itself blurs them: MW's *sura* entry ends with the flat note **`w.r. for svara`** — *sura* occurs in manuscripts as a **wrong reading for *svara***, in the Mahābhārata. Scribes confused the two words. A teacher standing on MW's "connected with 2. *svar*" and hearing Hindi *sur* has essentially every ingredient of the claim already assembled for him.

### V.3 The grammarians' own trope: catastrophe by being out of tune

There is one more anchor, and it is the most striking, because it puts *asura*-hood and *svara*-error side by side in the founding text of Sanskrit grammar. Patañjali's *Paspaśāhnika* — the introduction to the *Mahābhāṣya* — lists the reasons one must study grammar. Two consecutive items (`Balbudhi/prakriya: data/recension_mirror/mahabhasya/mahabhasya_raw.txt`, sections `[Pas_4.1]` and `[Pas_4.2]`):

> **[Pas_4.1]** *te **asurāḥ** helayaḥ helayaḥ iti kurvantaḥ parā babhūvuḥ | tasmāt brāhmaṇena na mlecchitavai na apabhāṣitavai | mlecchaḥ ha vai eṣaḥ yad apaśabdaḥ | mlecchā mā bhūmeti adhyeyaṃ vyākaraṇam.*
>
> "Those **asuras**, saying '*he 'layaḥ, he 'layaḥ*' [for *he 'rayaḥ*, 'O foes'], **were defeated**. Therefore a brahmin must not speak barbarously, must not speak corruptly. This — a corrupt word — is itself the barbarian. 'May we not be barbarians': for this, grammar is to be studied."

> **[Pas_4.2]** *duṣṭaḥ śabdaḥ **svarataḥ** varṇato vā mithyā prayukto na tam artham āha | sa vāg-vajro yajamānaṃ hinasti yathā **indraśatruḥ svarato 'parādhāt**.*
>
> "A corrupt word, wrongly employed **with respect to pitch-accent (*svara*)** or with respect to phoneme, does not convey its meaning. That word-thunderbolt destroys the sacrificer — as *indraśatruḥ* did, **through an error of *svara***."

Read them together. The asuras are destroyed **by defective speech**; and the very next thing said is that defective speech is defective **in *svara***, the paradigm case being a mispronunciation that destroyed its speaker. The *indraśatru* story's beneficiary was Tvaṣṭṛ's son Vṛtra — an asura.

And a final loop: the asuras' specific error was substituting **l for r** (*helayaḥ* for *herayaḥ*) — and Kātyāyana's vārttika on **A 8.2.18** lists **असुर** itself among the words in which *r* optionally becomes *l*: **असुरः, असुलः** (Kāśikā, `data/recension_mirror/kashika_offline.json`, `A_8.2.18`). The word for the people undone by saying *l* for *r* is itself a word that says *l* for *r*.

None of this is an etymology. But "the asura is the one whose speech is out of true, who errs in *svara*" is a thing the vyākaraṇa tradition genuinely says, in its most-read passage.

### V.4 The tradition's own account of how *sura* arose

The tradition was not unaware that *sura* is the odd one out. Its explanation is the churning-of-the-ocean story, which Apte quotes at *suraḥ*:

> ***surā**-pratigrahād devāḥ **surā** ity abhiviśrutāḥ* — "From accepting *surā* (liquor), the gods became renowned as *sura*s."
> (Apte, s.v. `suraH`, citing **Rām.** — Rāmāyaṇa, Bālakāṇḍa; the companion half-verse has the Daityas, who refused it, remembered as *a-surāḥ*.)

That is a folk etymology too. But it is a revealing one: it is the tradition itself reaching for an explanation of why there is a word *sura* at all, and reaching for one that does **not** treat *sura* as the base and *asura* as its negation. It derives *both* from *surā*, the liquor.

**Provenance caveat:** the Rāmāyaṇa is **not on disk**. Quoted here via Apte.

## VI. Verdict

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does the tradition derive *sura* from *svara*/"harmony"? | **No.** No grammatical, lexicographical, or commentarial source on disk does this. *Sura* is from √*su/sū* (Uṇādi 2.25) or *su-*√*rā* (Apte); *svara* is from √*svṛ*. Different roots. |
| Does the tradition ever read *asura* as *a-* + *sura*? | **Yes, as a secondary option.** Śaṅkara at BṛhU 1.3.1: *surebhyo vā devebhyo 'nyatvāt*. His primary gloss, though, is *asuṣu ramaṇāt*. |
| Does the tradition ever read *asura* as "not in a good state"? | **Yes** — the *Nirukta* analysis reported by Apte: *na suṣṭhu ratāḥ … sthāneṣu capalāḥ*, "not well-settled, unsteady in their stations." Note this splits *su-* as the prefix "well," not as the noun *sura*. |
| Is there a traditional link between asura-hood and being out of tune? | **Yes, but not etymologically** — Mahābhāṣya *Paspaśā*, where the asuras are destroyed by corrupt speech and the immediately following item is destruction by *svara*-error. |
| What is the historically correct derivation? | ***Asura* is primary** — √*as* + *uran* (Uṇādi 1.42), most plausibly connected with *asu*, "vital breath," and originally an honorific of Varuṇa, Savitṛ, Parjanya. ***Sura* is a back-formation** from it, by false analysis of the initial *a-* as the privative — exactly as *sita* ("white") was back-formed from *asita* ("black"). MW states this in both entries. |
| What settles it? | **RV 3.55**, whose twenty-two verses each close with *mahád devā́nām **ásuratvam** ékam* — "great and one is the **asura-hood of the gods**" — set against **zero** occurrences of *sura* meaning "god" in the entire Ṛgveda. |

**The honest summary for a reader.** Chinmayananda's *substance* — that *deva* and *asura* name two orientations of one person's own faculties, indexed to the guṇas, at war in every body beginninglessly — is squarely traditional and can be cited verbatim from Śaṅkara. His *etymology* is not traditional. It rests on a Hindi homophone (*sur* < *svara*), reinforced by a real but distinct proposal recorded in Monier-Williams (*sura* ~ *svar*, "light, heaven"), and it inverts the actual historical relation between the two words. It is best presented as what it is: an effective homiletic gloss with a genuine near-relative in the *Nirukta*'s *na suṣṭhu ratāḥ*, not as the derivation the grammarians give.

## VII. Gaps in the on-disk corpus

Four primaries bearing directly on this question are **absent from both repositories**, and three claims above rest on Apte's or MW's report of them rather than on the text:

1. **Yāska, *Nirukta*.** The *na suṣṭhu ratāḥ* analysis (§II.1) is quoted via Apte, with no chapter/section number. `data/articles/source/language-perspective-synthesis.md` already flags that the project's *Nirukta* OCR is of insufficient quality for verbatim citation. Acquiring the Sarup edition would close this and is the single highest-value acquisition for this topic.
2. **Vālmīki, *Rāmāyaṇa*, Bālakāṇḍa** (the *surā-pratigrahāt* verse, §V.4) — quoted via Apte.
3. **Śatapatha Brāhmaṇa** and the Brāhmaṇa literature generally — the *soḥ … asoḥ* creation etiology (§II.4) is unattributed in Apte and cannot be located without them.
4. **Amarakośa** and the *nighaṇṭu*/kośa literature — MW cites *Naighaṇṭuka* for *asura* = "cloud" and *asu* = *prajñā*; neither is checkable here.

Also worth noting: **Ujjvaladatta's *vṛtti* on the Uṇādi-sūtras** is present in `Balbudhi/prakriya` only as an unOCR'd 1873 scan (`data/recension_mirror/unadi/unadisutra_ujvaladatta_vritti_1873_text.pdf`, 5.9 MB), and no PDF text-extraction tooling is installed in this environment. Ujjvaladatta on Uṇādi 1.42 would be the single most authoritative statement of *which* √*as* the sūtra intends — the one point on which MW (√*as* 2, "to throw") and Apte (*asu*, "breath") diverge.
