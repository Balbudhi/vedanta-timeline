# *Asura* and *Sura*: What the Tradition Actually Derives

There is a gloss that circulates widely in modern Vedānta teaching, often attributed to Swami Chinmayananda: that *sura* is connected with *sur* — tone, harmony, being in tune — and that *asura* is therefore its negation, the one who is out of tune, discordant, inharmonious.

It is a beautiful teaching. The question is whether it is the derivation the Sanskrit tradition gives.

The short answer is that the specific etymology is not traditional, but almost everything else about the gloss is — and the traditional material is considerably richer, and stranger, than the popular version. Three things have to be separated, because they have different answers:

1. **What did Chinmayananda actually write?** Something else, as it turns out — and it is orthodox.
2. **What do the grammarians derive?** Not "harmony." But not "not-god" either. Both words come from roots meaning *to shine*.
3. **Is there anything in the tradition that connects asura-hood with being out of tune?** Yes — and it is far stronger than an etymology. It is a narrative that runs through two of the principal Upaniṣads.

---

## I. What Chinmayananda actually wrote

It is worth starting here, because the printed record is clear and it is not what the popular gloss says.

In his commentary on the *Viṣṇu Sahasranāma*, Chinmayananda has to gloss *sura* three separate times, because three of the Lord's thousand names are built on it. He gives the same derivation each time.

On name 85, **Sureśaḥ**:

> "The denizens of the Heavens are called in the Purāṇas as **Suras**. *Īśa* means the Lord; *Sureśa*, therefore, indicates the God of gods, the Lord of the Suras. **The gods are called as Suras because they are capable of blessing their devotees with a fulfilment of their desires.**"

with a footnote to the name, in full: *"And their opposites are called as Asuras."*

On name 286, **Sureśvaraḥ**, he makes the morphology explicit:

> "*Sura* means *Deva* and so the term indicates 'the God of all gods'. **The word *Sura* also can be dissolved to mean Giver (*Ra*) of Plenty (*Su*)**; *Sura* therefore stands for a person of extreme charity."

"Giver (*Ra*) of Plenty (*Su*)" is *su* + √*rā* — and it is precisely the derivation the Sanskrit lexicographical tradition gives. Śabdakalpadruma opens its entry on *suraḥ* with it:

> **सुष्ठु राति ददात्यभीष्टमिति** — *suṣṭhu rāti dadāty abhīṣṭam iti* — "he who **gives well** what is desired," *su* + √*rā* + *ka*.

Apte gives the same bracket, word for word. So on the page, Chinmayananda is not doing folk etymology at all. He is transmitting a classical *nirvacana* accurately, and citing it.

His treatment of the *devas* and *asuras* themselves is equally orthodox. Commenting on the Kena Upaniṣad's story of the gods' victory, he writes:

> "the Gods, meaning **the higher spiritual values of the life** (love, tolerance, patience, kindness, charity, piety, sympathy, etc.) having won a victory over the demons, meaning **the lower animal values of life** (hatred, prejudice, anger, jealousy, selfishness, egoism, vanity, etc.) with the help of the Eternal Self…"

And on *Surādhyakṣaḥ*: "When in the **Heaven of our bosom**, the thought-angels are threatened by the negative tendencies and criminal purposes…"

This — that *deva* and *asura* name two orientations inside one person — is the heart of the teaching, and it is not his invention. It is Śaṅkara's, verbatim (§V below).

In *The Holy Geeta*, his fullest treatment of the theme — the commentary on chapter 16, *daivāsura-sampad-vibhāga-yoga* — he classifies "the entire mankind, of all times and of all ages" under three types: "(a) **the Divinely Good** (Devas), (b) **the Diabolically Fallen** (Asuras) and (c) **the Incorrigibly Indifferent** (Rakshasas)." He offers no derivation of *asura* there at all.

**So the "harmony" gloss appears nowhere in his printed corpus** — not in *The Holy Geeta*, the *Viṣṇu Sahasranāma*, the *Vivekacūḍāmaṇi* talks, or the commentaries on the Kena, Īśāvāsya, Praśna, Aitareya, Taittirīya and Ātmabodha. Where he explains the word, he gives *su* + √*rā*.

### The idiom, however, is unmistakably his

What the printed corpus *does* show is that the musical metaphor for spiritual failure is Chinmayananda's own, used constantly — just never welded to the etymology. From *The Holy Geeta*:

> "An individual becomes incapable of facing the challenges of life and meeting its rising demands, because, in his false estimation of things and beings, **he comes to play out of tune with the whole orchestra of life**. … He, **who is capable of tuning himself up thus with the whole**, is the one who is marked out for sure success and complete victory in life."

> "Intemperance would bring discordant and riotous agitations in the various matter layers of the personality, **shattering the harmonious melody of integration**."

> "to a great musician, **to sing deliberately out of tune, in disharmonious notes**, is as difficult as it is for a beginner to sing correctly."

That is the whole gloss, in his voice, minus only the word *asura*.

### And so is the method

The other half of the gloss — splitting a word at a privative *a-* and reading the remainder morally — is likewise demonstrably his habit. In a recorded talk on the significance of the Rāmāyaṇa he runs the technique twice in fifteen seconds:

> "Thus we are told in **Ayodhyā** — *yudh*, conflict. **Ayodhyā: where there is no conflict.**"

> "To **Daśaratha** — **one who has conquered all the ten sense organs** — was born the Supreme Lord Śrī Rāma."

*A-yodhyā*, "not-to-be-fought," is exactly the *a-sura* move: prefix read as negation, remainder given an inward sense, the whole turned into a description of a state of mind. A teacher who derives *Ayodhyā* that way, describes the fallen life as "playing out of tune with the whole orchestra of life," and elsewhere glosses *sura* as *su* + √*rā*, needs one sentence in one talk to arrive at "the *asura* is the one out of tune."

So the attribution is entirely plausible, even though it remains unverified in print; and what he would have been doing is **homiletics, not grammar** — the same genre as *Ayodhyā*, "where there is no conflict," which is also not what the word historically means.

That is not a demotion. §IV shows the reading has excellent warrant of a different kind.

## II. What the grammarians derive

### II.1 Yāska: the two words are a matched pair, and the *a-* is not a negation

The oldest etymological authority in the tradition is Yāska's *Nirukta* (c. 5th century BCE or earlier). He treats *asura* at **3.8**, and gives four analyses in succession:

> **असुरा असुरताः स्थानेषु, अस्ता स्थानेभ्य इति वा ।**
> *asurā(ḥ) a-suratā(ḥ) sthāneṣu, astā(ḥ) sthānebhya iti vā*
> "*asuras*: **not well-settled** in their stations; **or**: **hurled** from their stations."

> **अपि वा असुर् इति प्राणनाम, अस्तः शरीरे भवति तेन तद्वन्तः ।**
> *api vā asur iti prāṇa-nāma, astaḥ śarīre bhavati tena tadvantaḥ*
> "**Or**: *asu* is a **name for *prāṇa***, the vital breath; it is deposited in the body; they are possessed of it."

> **सोर् देवान् असृजत तत् सुराणां सुरत्वम्, असोर् असुरान् असृजत तद् असुराणाम् असुरत्वम् इति विज्ञायते ।**
> *soḥ devān asṛjata tat surāṇāṃ suratvam, asor asurān asṛjata tad asurāṇām asuratvam iti vijñāyate*
> "**From *su*** — the excellent part of himself — **he emitted the devas: that is the *sura*-hood of the *sura*s. From *asu* he emitted the *asura*s: that is the *asura*-hood of the *asura*s.**"

Two of these deserve attention.

The **first** splits the word as *a-* + *su-* + *ratāḥ*: "**not well**-settled." The *su-* here is the ordinary prefix "well, good." This is a *nirvacana* of *asura* as *being-not-properly-ordered*, *unsteady*, off its proper station — from the tradition's founding etymologist. It is the closest classical relative of the harmony gloss, and it is genuinely close.

The **fourth** derives *sura* and *asura* **together**, from the ablatives *soḥ* and *asoḥ* — the same *-ra* formant on two contrasted stems, *su* ("the excellent") and *asu*. This matters: **the tradition has paired the two words since Yāska, but it did not do so by reading the *a-* as a privative.** The contrast is *su* versus *asu*, not *sura* versus *a-sura*.

Yāska returns to the word at **10.34**, glossing the Ṛgvedic refrain *mahad devānām asuratvam ekam*, and adds a fifth sense: *api vā asur iti prajñā-nāma* — "*asu* is a name for ***prajñā***, insight." On that reading *asura* means **"the one possessed of wisdom."**

### II.2 There is a real Sanskrit root *sur*, and it means "to shine"

This is the datum that changes the picture. Pāṇini's *Dhātupāṭha* — the canonical list of Sanskrit verbal roots — contains:

> **षुर~ ऐश्वर्यदीप्त्योः** — *ṣura~ aiśvarya-dīptyoḥ* (*tudādi* class, 6.66; present *surati*)
> "in the senses of ***aiśvarya*** (lordship) and ***dīpti*** (radiance)."

Both major dictionaries carry it. Monier-Williams: "*sur* … cl. 6 P. *surati*, **to rule, possess supreme or superhuman power**; **to shine**." Apte: "*sur* I. 6 P. (*surati*) 1 **To rule, govern.** 2 **To shine**." Durgādāsa, commenting on Vopadeva's *Kavikalpadruma*, glosses the root's two senses as *dīptiḥ* and *aiśvaryam*.

### II.3 *Sura* is a regular Pāṇinian formation: "the shining one"

Śabdakalpadruma gives three derivations of *suraḥ*. The first is the *su* + √*rā* one Chinmayananda uses. **The second is the significant one:**

> **यद्वा, सुरति शोभते इति । सुर + "इगुपधे"ति कः ।**
> *yadvā, surati śobhate iti | sura + "igupadhe"ti kaḥ*
> "**Or: *surati*, that is, HE SHINES** — from the root *sur* with the affix ***ka***, by [the sūtra beginning] *igupadha…*"

The sūtra invoked is real, and it is mainstream Pāṇini, not a marginal appendix:

> **इगुपधज्ञाप्रीकिरः कः** — *igupadha-jñā-prī-kiraḥ kaḥ* — **Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.1.135**

"The *kṛt* affix ***ka*** comes after a root having an *ik* vowel as its penultimate, and after √*jñā*, √*prī*, √*kṝ*." The root **सुर्** has *u* — an *ik* vowel — as its penultimate. It therefore takes *ka*, yielding **सुर**.

This is worth stating plainly, because it is usually missed: ***sura* is not a grammatical orphan.** It has a fully regular derivation, by an ordinary rule of the Aṣṭādhyāyī, from a root the Dhātupāṭha glosses as *lordship* and *radiance*. On this analysis:

> ***sura* = "the shining one," "the lordly one"** — the exact semantic twin of *deva*, which the tradition derives from √*div*, *dyotana*, "to shine."

### II.4 And *asura* is derived the same way

*Asura* is handled by **Uṇādi-sūtra 1.42, असेरुरन् (*aser uran*)**: root √*as* plus the affix *uran*, giving *asura* — morphologically a sibling of *śvaśura*, "father-in-law," and containing no privative *a-* whatever.

Which √*as*? The two great Sanskrit-to-Sanskrit lexica agree, and their answer is unexpected. Śabdakalpadruma lists four derivations:

> 1. *asyati devān kṣipati iti* — "he **hurls** the gods"
> 2. *yadvā na suraḥ, virodhe nañ-tatpuruṣaḥ* — "or: **not-*sura***, a *nañ*-compound in the sense of opposition"
> 3. *yadvā nāsti surā yasya saḥ* — "or: he who has no *surā*, liquor"
> 4. **सूर्य्यपक्षे असति दीप्यते इति उरन् ।** *sūrya-pakṣe: asati **dīpyate** iti uran* — "**in the sense of *the sun*: *asati*, that is, HE SHINES**"

and then adds: **सूर्य्यः । इति मेदिनी** — "**the sun.** So the *Medinī-kośa*."

Vācaspatyam gives the same and puts the shining sense **first**:

> **असुर पु० अस—दीप्तौ उर० । १ सूर्य्ये ।**
> *asura: asa—**dīptau** ura° | 1 sūrye*
> "*asura*, masc.: from √*as* **in the sense of *dīpti*, shining**, with *ura* — (1) **the SUN**."

### II.5 The upshot

| | root | sense of the root | affix | authority | a meaning given |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ***sura*** | √**सुर्** | *aiśvarya-**dīptyoḥ*** — lordship, **radiance** | *ka* | **Aṣṭādhyāyī 3.1.135** | god, **the sun**, sage |
| ***asura*** | √**अस्** | ***dīptau*** — **shining** | *uran* | Uṇādi 1.42 | **the sun**, spirit, demon |

**In the grammatical tradition, *sura* and *asura* are not opposites.** Both are derived from roots meaning "to shine," and the lexica give **"the sun"** as a meaning of each. The oppositional reading — *na suraḥ*, "not-a-*sura*" — is on record, but Śabdakalpadruma lists it as derivation *two of four*.

This is the deepest thing the investigation turns up. The popular gloss assumes the two words are a positive and its negation. The grammarians treat them as two independently derived words that happen to mean roughly the same thing.

## III. Pāṇini's own evidence

A scan of all 3,951 sūtras of the Aṣṭādhyāyī returns *asura* twice and *sura* never.

- **4.4.123 — असुरस्य स्वम्** (*asurasya svam*), "[the affix *yat*] after *asura* in the sense 'belonging to the asura'."
- **4.4.124 — मायायाम् अण्** (*māyāyām aṇ*), immediately following, giving *āsurī* specifically in the sense of *māyā*. The Kāśikā illustrates with the Vedic *āsurī māyā svadhayā kṛtāsi*.

So Pāṇini composed **a dedicated pair of rules for *asura*** and **never mentions *sura*** as a stem at all. A word that needs its own two sūtras is one the grammarian regards as basic; a word absent from the entire grammar was not yet doing significant work in his language.

Two further details from the same tradition, both of which return below:

**(a) *Asura* is the name of a people.** In the Kāśikā's *parśvādi* list — a roster of *āyudhajīvi-saṅgha*, "confederations that live by arms," attached to Aṣṭādhyāyī 5.3.117 — *asura* appears in plain company: *parśu, **asura**, rakṣas, bāhlīka, vayas, marut, daśārha, piśāca, viśāla, satvat, vasu*. Monier-Williams records the same: "N. of a warrior-tribe." In the grammarians' world *asura* names a **tribe**, sitting beside *marut* and *satvat* — not a metaphysical antonym.

**(b) The word itself undergoes *r* → *l*.** Kātyāyana's vārttika on Aṣṭādhyāyī 8.2.18 lists the words in which *r* optionally becomes *l*: *vāla, mūla, laghu, **asura**, alam, aṅguli*. Hence both **असुरः** and **असुलः**.

## IV. The real ground for "harmony": the *udgītha-brāhmaṇa*

Here the popular gloss turns out to be standing on something solid — but it is not an etymology. It is a story, told twice, in the two longest and most authoritative Upaniṣads. In it, the conflict between *devas* and *asuras* is decided **by singing**, and the *devas* are defined as **those who entered *svara***.

### The war is a singing contest

> *devāsurā ha vai yatra saṃyetire | ubhaye prājāpatyās tad dha devā **udgītham** ājahrur anenainān abhibhaviṣyāma iti*
> "When the devas and asuras contended — both being descendants of Prajāpati — the devas **took up the *udgītha*** [the sung portion of the Sāman], thinking: 'by this we shall overcome them.'"
> — **Chāndogya Upaniṣad 1.2.1**

> *te ha devā ūcur hantāsurān yajña **udgīthenātyayāma** iti*
> "The devas said: 'Come, let us overcome the asuras in the sacrifice **by means of the *udgītha***.'"
> — **Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.3.1**

The asuras pierce each sense-faculty with evil in turn; but when they strike at the breath in the mouth — the one that sings —

> *taṃ hāsurā ṛtvā vidadhvaṃsur yathāśmānam ākhaṇam ṛtvā vidhvaṃseta*
> "the asuras, striking against it, **were shattered** — as a clod is shattered striking against a rock."
> — **Chāndogya 1.2.7**; and Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3.7, *tato devā abhavan, parāsurā*, "then they became devas, and the asuras were defeated."

### The śruti *names* the breath "svara"

Two sections later, the Chāndogya gives the vital breath a name outright:

> ***स्वर** इतीमम् आचक्षते, **स्वर** इति **प्रत्यास्वर** इत्यमुम् ।*
> *svara itīmam ācakṣate, svara iti pratyāsvara ity amum*
> "**This one** [the breath] they call ***svara***; and **that one** [the sun] ***pratyāsvara***."
> — **Chāndogya 1.3.2**

Śaṅkara explains the name from the root √*svṛ*: *yasmāt prāṇaḥ **svaraty** eva na punar mṛtaḥ pratyāgacchati* — "because the breath goes forth, and does not return once departed; the sun, having set, returns day after day, hence *pratyā-svara*."

### The devas become immortal by entering *svara*

> *te nu vittvordhvā ṛcaḥ sāmno yajuṣaḥ **svaram eva prāviśan***
> "Having understood [Death's intent], the devas rose up out of the Ṛc, the Sāman and the Yajus, and **entered *svara* itself**."
> — **Chāndogya 1.4.3**

> *eṣa u **svaro** yad etad akṣaram etad **amṛtam abhayam** | **tat praviśya devā amṛtā abhavan***
> "This is the ***svara***: this imperishable syllable, **immortal, fearless**. **Entering it, the devas became immortal.**"
> — **Chāndogya 1.4.4**

Śaṅkara, commenting: *tad apāsyāmṛtābhaya-guṇam akṣaraṃ **svaraṃ svara-śabditam** prāviśann eva* — "abandoning that, they entered the imperishable, **called *svara***, whose qualities are immortality and fearlessness."

And when the chant is traced to its root in the dialogue of Śilaka and Dālbhya:

> "'***Svara***,' he said. — 'And what is the goal of *svara*?' — '***Prāṇa***,' he said. For *svara* is produced by *prāṇa*."
> — **Chāndogya 1.8–1.9**

### *Svara* is the wealth and the gold of the chant

The Bṛhadāraṇyaka, in the very chapter that opens with the deva/asura war, ends its analysis of the Sāman this way:

> *tasya vai **svara eva svam** | tasmād ārtvijyaṃ kariṣyan vāci **svaram iccheta** | … tasmād yajñe **svaravantaṃ** didṛkṣanta eva*
> "Of that [chant], ***svara* alone is its wealth**. Therefore one about to perform priestly office **should seek *svara* in his speech**. … Therefore at the sacrifice people desire to see **the one who has *svara***."
> — **Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3.25**

Śaṅkara: ***svara** iti kaṇṭha-gataṃ **mādhuryam**, tad evāsya svaṃ vibhūṣaṇam* — "***Svara*** means the **sweetness residing in the throat**; that alone is its ornament."

> *tasya vai **svara eva suvarṇam*** — "Of that, ***svara* alone is its gold**."
> — **Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3.26**

### The grammarians say it too

Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* — the founding text of Sanskrit grammatical commentary — opens by listing the reasons one must study grammar. Two consecutive items:

> *te **asurāḥ** helayaḥ helayaḥ iti kurvantaḥ parā babhūvuḥ*
> "Those **asuras**, saying *he 'layaḥ, he 'layaḥ* [for *he 'rayaḥ*, 'O foes'], **were defeated**."

> *duṣṭaḥ śabdaḥ **svarataḥ** varṇato vā mithyā prayukto na tam artham āha | sa **vāg-vajro** yajamānaṃ hinasti yathā **indraśatruḥ svarato 'parādhāt***
> "A corrupt word, wrongly employed **with respect to pitch-accent (*svara*)** or with respect to phoneme, does not convey its meaning. That **word-thunderbolt** destroys the sacrificer — as *indraśatruḥ* did, **through an error of *svara***."

The asuras are destroyed **by defective speech**; and the very next item says that defective speech is defective **in *svara***, the paradigm case being a mispronunciation that destroyed the man who uttered it. The victim of that famous accent error was Vṛtra — whom Yāska identifies as *tvāṣṭro 'suraḥ*, "the asura son of Tvaṣṭṛ."

There is even a closing loop. The asuras' specific error was substituting **l for r** — and, as noted above, *asura* is itself on Kātyāyana's list of words in which *r* becomes *l*. The name of the people undone by saying *l* for *r* is itself a word that says *l* for *r*.

### What this adds up to

*Prāṇa*, the vital breath, is the hinge, and both words hang from it. Breath rightly deployed **is** *svara*, **is** the Sāman, **is** the *udgītha* — the devas' weapon against the asuras, their refuge from death, the wealth and the gold of the chant. Breath turned to its own outward gratification is precisely what Śaṅkara means by *asura*.

So **"being in *svara*" versus "revelling in one's own *asu*s" is the deva/asura distinction**, stated in śruti and in Śaṅkara, with no pun required. Anyone who has taught the Chāndogya and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka — as Chinmayananda did — has this material to hand. "The asura is the one who is out of tune" is a fair compression of it. It is only when restated as a claim about *word-derivation* that it fails.

## V. Śaṅkara: the psychological reading, which *is* traditional

The substance of the modern teaching — that *deva* and *asura* name two dispositions inside one person — is not a modern accommodation. It is Śaṅkara's, and he states it more sharply than his modern successors do.

Commenting on the opening of the Chāndogya's story, he gives both etymologies at once:

> *devā **dīvyater dyotanārthasya** śāstrodbhāsitā indriya-vṛttayaḥ | asurās tad-viparītāḥ **sveṣv evāsuṣu** viṣvag-viṣayāsu prāṇana-kriyāsu **ramaṇāt** svābhāvikyas tama-ātmikā indriya-vṛttaya eva*
>
> "The **devas** are the sense-functions **illumined by scripture** — from √*div* in the sense of **shining**. The **asuras** are their opposite: the natural, darkness-natured sense-functions themselves, **from revelling in their own *asu*s**, in the outward-turned, object-directed activities of breathing."

His **primary** gloss of *asura* is *asuṣu ramaṇāt* — Yāska's third analysis (*asu* = *prāṇa*) turned into a moral psychology. Commenting on the parallel passage in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka he repeats it, and adds a second option: *surebhyo vā devebhyo 'nyatvāt*, "**or** because they are other than the *sura*s." So the *a-sura* reading *is* in the tradition — recorded second, as an alternative, and built on by nobody.

On the Chāndogya story he adds:

> *anyonyābhibhavodbhava-rūpaḥ saṃgrāma iva sarva-prāṇiṣu **pratideham** devāsura-saṅgrāmo **'nādi-kāla-pravṛtta** ity abhiprāyaḥ*
> "The purport is that this deva-asura war, in the form of the mutual overpowering and rising of each, goes on **in every body**, in all living beings, having commenced **from beginningless time**."

And in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka, commenting on Prajāpati's threefold instruction, he states the whole modern teaching outright:

> *athavā **na devā asurā vānye kecana vidyante manuṣyebhyaḥ** | … itarāṃś ca guṇān **sattva-rajas-tamāṃsy** apekṣya*
> "Or rather: **there are no devas or asuras whatever apart from human beings.** … Among humans, those endowed with excellent qualities are the devas; those in whom greed predominates are the humans; those bent on violence, the cruel, are the asuras — by reference to **sattva, rajas, and tamas**."
> — Śaṅkara on Bṛhadāraṇyaka 5.2.3

Two further passages are worth having. On **Īśā Upaniṣad 3**, *asuryā nāma te lokāḥ*, Śaṅkara notes that the category is relative, not a fixed species: *paramātma-bhāvam advayam apekṣya **devādayo 'py asurāḥ*** — "with reference to the non-dual state of the supreme Self, **even the gods are asuras**." And **Chāndogya 8.8.5** defines the *āsura* position **doctrinally** rather than by temperament — *asurāṇāṃ hy eṣopaniṣat*, "for this is the secret doctrine of the asuras": that the body is the self.

Chinmayananda's substantive teaching is therefore not merely defensible; it is quotable, verbatim, from the most authoritative commentary in Advaita.

## VI. The historical layer

One more question is worth separating out, because scholarship answers it differently from the tradition, and the two answers are not actually in conflict.

**In the Ṛgveda, *asura* is ancient, frequent, and overwhelmingly honorific.** It is a title of the highest gods:

> *kṣáyann asmábhyam **ásura** prácetā rā́jann énāṁsi śiśrathaḥ kṛtā́ni* — RV 1.24.14, of **Varuṇa**
> *hiraṇyahasto **ásuraḥ** sunīthaḥ sumṛḷīkaḥ svávām̐ yātv arvā́ṅ* — RV 1.35.10, of **Savitṛ**
> *arvā́ṅ eténa stanayitnúnehy apó niṣiñcánn **ásuraḥ** pitā́ naḥ* — RV 5.83.6, of **Parjanya**

The hostile sense exists, but it is later and explicitly marked — *anāyudhā́so **ásurā** **ádevāś***, "weaponless asuras, **godless**" (RV 8.96.9). **The Ṛgvedic opposition is *asura* versus *deva*. It is never *asura* versus *sura*.**

**More decisively, *asura*-hood is a property of the gods.** The hymn RV 3.55 has twenty-two verses, and every one of them closes on the same line:

> ***mahád devā́nām ásuratvam ékam*** — "Great and one is the ***asura*-hood of the gods**."

Beside it stands *asurya* in the same positive sense: *ná vā́ u yoṣad rudrā́d **asuryàm***, "nor does **asura-lordship** depart from Rudra" (RV 2.33.9). Yāska glosses this very refrain, and takes *asuratva* to be a virtue. A word cannot simultaneously be the negation of "god" and an abstract excellence attributed to the gods in a hymn's refrain.

**And *sura* does not occur in the Ṛgveda at all.** A full tokenization of every *sur-* form in the text yields zero instances of *sura* meaning "god." Every *sur-* token is either a *su-* compound of some other word (*su-ratha*, *su-rādhas*, *su-rūpa*, *su-raṇa*) or **surā́**, the liquor. The Ṛgvedic word for a god is *deva*.

Monier-Williams draws the conclusion in the dictionary entries themselves:

> *sura* m. (prob. fr. *asura* as if fr. *a-sura*, and as *sita* fr. *a-sita*) — a god, divinity
> *asura* — [In later Sanskrit *sura* has been formed from *asura*, as *sita* from *asita*.]

That is, *sura* is a **back-formation**: later speakers re-analysed the *a-* of *asura* as the privative prefix and extracted a positive counterpart that had never existed — exactly as *sita*, "white," was manufactured from *asita*, "black."

**How this sits with §II.** These accounts answer different questions and can both stand. Historically, *asura* is primary and *sura* is secondary. Traditionally, the grammarians — who were not doing historical linguistics — derived *sura* independently and regularly from √*sur*, "to shine," and Yāska paired the two from *su*/*asu* with no privative at all. What the popular gloss gets wrong is not the pairing, which is ancient, but the *direction*: it makes *sura* the base and *asura* its negation, and on every account available — historical, Yāska's, or Pāṇini's — that is backwards.

The tradition was aware that *sura* was the odd one out, and offered its own explanation: the churning of the ocean.

> ***surāḥ** pratigrahād devāḥ **surā** ity abhiviśrutāḥ | apratigrahaṇāt tasya daiteyāś ca **asurāḥ** smṛtāḥ*
> "From accepting *surā* (liquor), the gods became renowned as ***sura*s**; and from not accepting it, the Daiteyas are remembered as ***asura*s**."
> — quoted from the Rāmāyaṇa by Śabdakalpadruma

That is a folk etymology too. But it is a revealing one: it is the tradition itself reaching for an account of why the word *sura* exists at all — and reaching for one that derives **both** from *surā*, rather than treating *sura* as basic.

## VII. Verdict

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Did Chinmayananda write that *sura* comes from *svara*, "tone"? | **Not in his printed commentaries.** Where he explains the word, he gives *su* + √*rā*, "**Giver (*Ra*) of Plenty (*Su*)**" — which is the classical derivation, correctly transmitted. |
| Does any traditional source derive *sura* from *svara*? | **No.** *Svara* is from √*svṛ*, "to sound"; *sura* is from √*sur*, "to shine," or from *su* + √*rā*. Different roots. |
| Is *sura* then a grammatical orphan? | **No.** Śabdakalpadruma derives it by an ordinary rule of the Aṣṭādhyāyī — **3.1.135, *igupadhajñāprīkiraḥ kaḥ*** — from √*sur*, *aiśvarya-dīptyoḥ*. ***Sura* = "the shining one."** |
| What is the traditional derivation of *asura*? | Several. Uṇādi 1.42, √*as* + *uran* — where Śabdakalpadruma and Vācaspatyam both read the root as *dīptau*, "**to shine**," and give "**the sun**" as its meaning. Yāska adds *asu* = *prāṇa* (breath) and *asu* = *prajñā* (insight). *Na suraḥ*, "not-*sura*," is on the list, ranked second of four. |
| So are they opposites in the grammatical tradition? | **No.** Both are derived from roots meaning "to shine," and the lexica give "the sun" as a meaning of each. |
| Does the tradition read *asura* as "not in a good state"? | **Yes** — Yāska, *Nirukta* 3.8: *a-su-ratāḥ sthāneṣu*, "**not well-settled** in their stations." The closest classical relative of the popular gloss. |
| Is there a traditional link between asura-hood and being out of tune? | **Yes — strong, but narrative rather than etymological.** The devas defeat the asuras **by singing** (Chāndogya 1.2, Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3); the śruti **names the breath "*svara*"** (Chāndogya 1.3.2); **"entering *svara*, the devas became immortal"** (Chāndogya 1.4.4); ***svara* is the wealth and the gold of the chant** (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 1.3.25–26); and in the *Mahābhāṣya* the asuras are destroyed by corrupt speech, with an error of *svara* named as a thunderbolt that kills. |
| What is the historically correct account? | ***Asura* is primary** — an honorific of Varuṇa, Savitṛ and Parjanya, with *asura*-hood predicated of the gods themselves twenty-two times in RV 3.55. ***Sura* is a back-formation** from it; it does not occur in the Ṛgveda. |

**In sum.** The teaching is sound and the etymology is not, but the gap between them is much narrower than it first appears. Chinmayananda's substance — that *deva* and *asura* name two orientations of one person's own faculties, indexed to the *guṇa*s, at war in every body beginninglessly — is straight Śaṅkara, and his printed derivation of *sura* is the classical *su* + √*rā*. The "harmony" framing, wherever it originates, is a fair compression of the *udgītha-brāhmaṇa*, where the gods win by song and are named for entering *svara*; and it has a real classical relative in Yāska's *a-su-ratāḥ*, "not well-settled." What does not survive is only the narrow morphological claim — that Sanskrit *sura* comes from *svara*, "tone."

And the replacement is not a loss. The grammarians derive *sura* from √*sur*, "to shine, to hold sway" — which yields, instead of "the one in tune," something a Vedāntin should like at least as well:

> ***sura*: the radiant one.**
