# Madhva’s challenge to non-objectifying Advaita

## Question and scope

The claim being tested is not that the empirical person—the body, ego, or
individual agent—is Viṣṇu. It is the stricter Advaitic claim that, on removal
of that mistaken identification, the Self is Brahman and that every distinction
has no independent being outside Brahman. Madhva's central texts are unusually
well suited to this test because they argue specifically about the liberated
and witnessing self, not only about an embodied ego.

This is a control reading of selected passages from Madhva's *Anuvyākhyāna*
and his short *Māyāvāda-khaṇḍana*, set against two securely attributed
Śaṅkara passages. It does not claim to reconstruct every work in the
*Sarvamūla* corpus.

## 1. Madhva’s positive commitment

In *Anuvyākhyāna* 1.4.110–12, Madhva defines the manifest world through five
differences:

> *jīveśvarabhidā caiva jaḍeśvarabhidā tathā / jīvabhedo mithaś caiva
> jaḍajīvabhidā tathā // mithaś ca jaḍabhedo yaḥ prapañco bhedapañcakaḥ //*

> “The world is the fivefold difference: difference of jīva and Lord, of
> insentient [matter] and Lord, of jīvas from one another, of matter and jīva,
> and of material things from one another.”

The next verse calls this fivefold difference **satya** (“real”) and **anādi**
(“beginningless”) and calls the doctrine “duality does not exist” the view of
the unknowing. The noun *bhidā* is an abstract from √*bhid*, “split,
distinguish”; the compound *jīveśvara-bhidā* is plainly a distinction
*between* jīva and Īśvara, not merely an empirical contrast within a single
self.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 1.4.110–12](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:1298).

This is not a doctrine of two independent ultimates. Madhva calls Viṣṇu alone
*svatantra* (“independent”) even in liberation, and calls the Lord the giver of
liberation precisely because a dependent (*paratantra*) being cannot bestow it
by itself. His disagreement with Advaita is therefore not over whether finite
things are independent. It is over whether dependence entails non-difference
of essential nature.

Sources: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.3.94](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2910) and [3.3.16](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:3641).

## 2. The *Māyāvāda-khaṇḍana* makes the target explicit

Madhva's short polemical treatise opens its argument with the technical
expression **brahmātmaikya**:

> *na hi brahmātmaikyasya yāthārthyaṃ tatpakṣe / advaitahāneḥ
> svarūpātireke; anatireke svaprakāśatvād ātmanaḥ siddhasādhanatā.*

> “For the factual truth of Brahman–Self identity does not obtain on that
> position: if there is an otherness of essential nature, non-duality is lost;
> if there is no such otherness, then, since the Self is self-luminous, the
> claim proves what is already established.”

The syntax is compressed, but the alternatives are clear. **yāthārthya** is
“factuality” or “being as the thing truly is”; **svarūpa-atireka** is an
otherness of essential nature; and **siddha-sādhana** is the Nyāya fault of
trying to establish what is already established. Madhva is not here rebutting
only the equation of an embodied ego with Viṣṇu. He asks whether a real
*brahman–ātman* identity can be asserted at all. His answer is no: actual
otherness yields duality; no otherness yields a redundant identity-claim.

That is a serious objection to the user's ultimate formulation, but it
contains Madhva's own framing premise: that the Advaitic claim must either
assert an identity between terms possessing a fixed *svarūpa* or collapse into
a tautology. An Advaitin can deny that dichotomy by treating the empirical
*jīva* as superimposition and “Self” in *brahmātmaikya* as Brahman itself.
Madhva's further *Anuvyākhyāna* arguments show that he explicitly rejects
that reply; it should not be dismissed as though he had not heard it.

Source: [*Māyāvāda-khaṇḍana*](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/madhva_mayavada_khandana_anandamakaranda.txt:6).

## 3. The claim he directly denies

Madhva states:

> *nānyad anyatvam āpannaṃ kvacid dṛṣṭaṃ kathañcana /
> ato jīvasya na brahmabhāvaḥ syād dhi kadācana //*

> Madhva’s inferential conclusion: a jīva cannot ever have *brahmabhāva*, the
> state/nature of Brahman.

Here **brahmabhāvaḥ** is *brahma* + *bhāva* (“the state/nature of Brahman”);
**jīvasya** is genitive singular, “of the jīva”; and **kadācana** intensifies
the negation, “at any time whatsoever.” The first half is a compressed argument
from **anyatva** (“otherness/distinctness”) and **āpanna** (the past participle
of *ā-√pad*, “having attained”). Its syntactic ellipsis should not be made to
carry more than Madhva’s explicit conclusion: he rejects the inference that a
distinct jīva can be identical in essential nature with Brahman.

This passage is not defeated by saying that the body-mind is not Brahman.
Madhva’s conclusion concerns *jīva* itself. Nor does he present it as a
provisional discipline for the unawakened. His account of liberation retains
distinction: “given the liberated person's apprehension of difference, how
could there be non-difference?”

Sources: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.1.85–86](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:1595) and [2.3.38–39](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2733).

## 4. His strongest epistemic argument

The most important passage for a realization-based Advaita is not a simple
appeal to ordinary perception. Madhva explicitly frames the issue through the
purified witness:

> *śuddhaḥ sākṣī yadā siddho duḥkhitvaṃ vāryate katham /
> upajīvyapramāṇaṃ tad bhedagrāhakam eva hi /
> ato jīveśayor bhedaḥ śrutisāmarthyasusthiraḥ //*

> “When a pure witness is established, how is [the experience of] suffering to
> be denied? For the proof on which one relies is itself a grasper of
> difference. Therefore the difference of jīva and Lord is firmly established
> by the force of scripture.”

**upajīvya-pramāṇam** means the “presupposed/relied-on means of knowledge”:
*upajīvya* is the gerundive of *upa-√jīv*, “to be depended upon,” and
*bheda-grāhaka* means “that which apprehends difference.” Madhva’s point is
not merely that one happens to see difference before enlightenment. He argues
that the very evidence Advaita needs to establish a pure witnessing self also
discloses distinction, so Advaita cannot retain the former while cancelling the
latter without undermining its own epistemic basis.

The preceding argument prevents an easy reduction of **sākṣī** to the
body-mind ego. Madhva says that the relevant distinctions are all objects of
witness-cognition (*sākṣi-pratyaya-gocarāḥ*) and that, for all recognized
practice and transaction to be possible, the one witness must be accepted as
non-defective (*sākṣī nirdoṣa eva*). He then asks what becomes of suffering
once this **pure** witness is established. The argument is therefore aimed at
the enduring knowing subject as Madhva understands it—not at a merely worldly
psychological “I.”

That is a genuine argument, and it cannot fairly be described as a refutation
only of the proposition “the ego is God.” Its force, however, depends on a
Madhva premise: witness-cognition and scripture’s difference-statements are
finally veridical in the same, unqualified sense. Advaita denies that premise:
it treats the witness as self-revealing, but the object/agent/Lord distinctions
within cognition as sublatable when they are taken to be independently real.
The disagreement is therefore principled. Madhva’s argument does not refute
Advaita without assuming the epistemic finality that Advaita rejects; Advaita
does not escape it merely by renaming all difference “appearance.” It owes an
account of why the witness’s apparent grasp of difference does not survive as a
second reality.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.3.63–69](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2810).

## 5. Madhva’s interpretation of non-difference language

Madhva does not ignore *abheda* texts. He gives them restricted readings:

> *ato ’bhedavade vaitāḥ śrutayaḥ pravadanti hi …
> sādṛśyāc ca pradhānatvāt svātantryād api cābhidām /
> āhur īśena jīvasya na svarūpābhidāṃ kvacit //*

> “Thus these texts speak as if of non-difference. They speak of non-difference
> between the Lord and jīva because of likeness, primacy, and dependence—not
> of non-difference in essential nature, ever.”

**sādṛśya** is likeness; **pradhānatva** is primacy; and **svātantrya** is
independence. In **abhidām**, the initial *a-* is privative—“non-difference,”
not the unrelated noun *abhidā* (“denotation”); the sandhi in *api cābhidām*
does not mark the boundary. Thus **svarūpābhidā** means non-difference in
essential nature, precisely what Madhva denies. This is the direct Madhva
answer to an asymmetrical “all is Brahman in being” formulation. He can accept
dependence on the independent Lord and a scriptural language of intimacy; he
refuses the inference from dependence to essential non-difference.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.3.67–70](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2824).

## 6. Direct realization does not remove the disagreement

Madhva explicitly permits immediate realization: *āparokṣyaṃ bhaved viṣṇoḥ*
(“there is immediate realization of Viṣṇu”) through steadfast devotion and
*upāsanā*. Yet he simultaneously says that Viṣṇu alone remains independent and
that difference does not disappear in liberation. Thus an appeal to direct
experience, by itself, cannot decide the dispute. Madhva has a model of direct
knowledge that is devotional, immediate, and non-illusory without becoming
identity.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 3.3.1–4](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:3595).

## 7. The precise point of collision with Śaṅkara

Śaṅkara's own argument is not the claim that an independent empirical ego is
Viṣṇu. In *Brahma-sūtra-bhāṣya* 2.1.14, he says that the effect-world is
ultimately non-other than its cause because it is absent apart from the cause
(*kāraṇāt … ananyatvaṃ vyatirekeṇa abhāvaḥ kāryasya*); a modification has no
separate entity-status in the way a pot does over and above clay. In the same
discussion, Śaṅkara makes the non-negotiable limit explicit: unity alone is
ultimately real; plurality is the expansion of false cognition. His
*Chāndogya* commentary then gives the formula most relevant here: all name and
form is true **with Sat as its Self** (*sadātmanā*), while as other than Sat it
is untrue (*sato ’nyatvena anṛtatvam*).

Sources: [Śaṅkara on *Brahma-sūtra* 2.1.14](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/shankara_brahma_sutra_bhasya.txt:2426) and [Śaṅkara on *Chāndogya* 6.3.2](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedic/chandogya_upanisad_gretil.txt:6914).

That is a real Advaitic answer to Madhva, not a change of subject. But Madhva
has a reply to exactly its mechanism: he argues that sublation (*bādha*) cannot
establish non-being, because only something existing can be sublated—“a barren
woman's son is not killed; Yajñadatta is killed.” The two systems thus differ
at the level of what sublation and dependence can establish. Śaṅkara infers
non-otherness from the effect's lack of a separate being; Madhva infers the
reality of a dependent difference from the very cognition that Advaita calls
sublatable. Neither inference follows from a neutral grammatical fact alone.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.3.44–48](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2754).

## 8. He does address *mithyā* and the “indescribable” category

Madhva records the familiar dream-style inference—“the disputed [world] is
*mithyā* because it is perceived, like a dream”—and attacks its logical form.
He argues that if the dream-example is itself *mithyā*, the conclusion is
already assumed; if it is not, the proposed pervasion fails. He then argues
from the witness's cognition and distinguishes a stable cognition from a
merely appearance-like one. This is not a decisive refutation of Advaita, but
it shows that he is engaging an inference for *mithyātva*, not only the crude
claim that the manifest world is sheer nonexistence.

Likewise, in the immediately preceding sequence he explicitly introduces
**anirvācyatva** (“indescribability”) as a possible residual category and
rejects it on his own pramāṇa theory. The critical question is therefore not
whether Madhva knew there was an Advaitic distinction between *sat* and *asat*.
It is whether his premise—that any determinate object of cognition must be
finally real and difference-bearing—is sound. Advaita says no; Madhva says that
the denial makes the very cognition and scripture Advaita relies upon
unintelligible.

Sources: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.2.217–21](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2351) and [2.3.42–43](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2745).

## 9. Non-objectification is not an easy escape from Madhva

Madhva also blocks the thought that *bheda* applies only to two already
objectified things. He writes: *svarūpaṃ vastuno bhedo yanna tasya grahe
grahaḥ*—difference is the essential nature of a thing; without it, the thing
is not grasped in being grasped. This is a thesis about what a thing is, not
only about a relation subsequently imposed by a finite knower. On Madhva's
account, the jīva–Īśvara distinction therefore survives the removal of bodily
identification and the purification of knowing.

The user’s appeal to a non-objectifying Self is still a coherent Advaitic
move: it denies that the final Self is one more object with a bounded
*svarūpa*. But it is not a point Madhva simply overlooks. His contention is
that an alleged consciousness without real differentiating character cannot
support cognition, scripture, or liberation. The dispute is now exactly
located: Advaita denies that final reality is a set of distinct *vastu*s at
all; Madhva affirms that difference is intrinsic to the real jīva and Lord,
including the liberated knower.

Source: [*Anuvyākhyāna* 2.2.222–26](/Users/eeshan/Dev/vedanta-timeline/data/sources/sanskrit/vedanta/full_corpus/madhva_anuvyakhyana_gretil.txt:2368).

## Finding

Madhva does **not** refute the limited claim that the empirical agent is not
Viṣṇu, that no finite thing is independent, or that realization transforms the
way the world is apprehended. He agrees that Viṣṇu alone is independent and
insists more strongly than many Advaitin caricatures that the world is real.

He does directly reject the user’s ultimate claim if “I am Brahman” means that
the purified Self and Brahman are non-different in *svarūpa*. He denies this
of the liberated jīva, interprets *abheda* as likeness/primacy/dependence rather
than identity, and makes the persistence of difference a condition of his
account of knowledge and liberation. Calling his target only a vulgar Advaita
would therefore be inaccurate.

The fair conclusion is not that one side has been textually eliminated. It is
that the two systems make incompatible final claims about what dependence is.
For Śaṅkara, the effect’s absence apart from its ground yields non-otherness;
for Madhva, dependence preserves a real, eternal difference of essential
nature. A synthetic philosophy may use Madhva’s realism and his warning against
the divinization of the empirical ego. It must not claim that Madhva’s own
argument leaves ultimate *ātman–Brahman* non-difference untouched.
