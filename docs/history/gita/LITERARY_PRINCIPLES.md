# Literary and translation-theory principles for a lossless English Gītā

Research input (2026-11-08), subordinate to the owner's principles and to Sri Aurobindo's
teaching on English (`ENGLISH_CRAFT.md`). Authors appear only as reference points for
principles, cited from scholarship or widely quoted statements; nothing here studies or
imitates an author's text. **[V]** wording confirmed in at least one source; **[P]**
paraphrase or unconfirmed wording. Quotations must be checked against printed editions
before any public use.

## 1. English word-formation: legitimate and legible coinage

- **Right-hand head rule.** An English compound names a kind of its last element
  ("blueberry" is a berry); a coinage obeying it is readable on first sight, one that does
  not must be learned (Williams 1981; KU working paper; Osijek on exocentric compounds).
- **Transparency is graded per constituent.** Transparent modifier and head each make a
  compound easier to read (Libben; eye-tracking, PMC5862730).
- **The relation between parts is unstated; the reader recovers it.** Downing (*Language*
  53, 1977): novel compounds get many readings without context; Levi (1978) and Gagné &
  Shoben's CARIN (1997) catalogue the relations. Consequence: context or a card must fix
  the relation — or, where the Sanskrit compound is itself relation-ambiguous, the English
  compound can keep that ambiguity on purpose.
- **Nonce → institutionalized → lexicalized** (Bauer, *English Word-Formation*, 1983, ch. 3)
  [P]. Every coinage starts as a nonce word; to become a term for the reader it is defined
  once on its card and used the same way every time.
- **Native Germanic resource and its limit.** The Old English kenning (whale-road, bone-house);
  Snorri's *nykrat* ("made monstrous") as the native rule against incoherent compounds [P];
  William Barnes's Saxon coinages (speechcraft, birdlore) show both the reach and the failure
  of systematic purism; Hopkins: "it is the vice of distinctiveness to become queer" (letter
  to Bridges, 1879) [P].
- **Blends vs compounds.** A blend cuts and merges forms (the *Finnegans Wake* type) and is far
  harder to analyse than a compound of intact morphemes. For a checkable translation the
  compound is the tool; a blend only rarely, and only with a card.

## 2. Translation theory

- **Dryden (1680):** metaphrase, paraphrase, imitation; metaphrase rejected as crabbed. Our
  design is metaphrase made readable by the other layers.
- **Schleiermacher (1813):** "Entweder der Uebersezer läßt den Schriftsteller möglichst in
  Ruhe, und bewegt den Leser ihm entgegen" [V] — a lossless design moves the reader toward
  the author; correct but slightly estranged English.
- **Benjamin (1923):** "Die Interlinearversion des heiligen Textes ist das Urbild oder Ideal
  aller Übersetzung" [V]; literalness as an arcade through which the original shows; render
  the *Art des Meinens* (way of meaning), not only what is meant.
- **Nida (1964):** formal equivalence and the gloss translation — keep "holy kiss", explain
  in a note [V]. The line-plus-note split in its classic form.
- **Newmark (1981):** semantic translation renders "as closely as the semantic and syntactic
  structures of the second language allow, the exact contextual meaning of the original" [V].
- **Nabokov (1955; Onegin 1964):** literal translation = "as closely as the associative and
  syntactical capacities of another language allow, the exact contextual meaning of the
  original" [V]; "The clumsiest literal translation is a thousand times more useful than the
  prettiest paraphrase" [V]; footnotes "reaching up like skyscrapers" [V-fragment]; he
  sacrificed euphony and even grammar [P]. Edmund Wilson (1965) attacked his rare words
  ("mollitude", "rememorate") as affectation.
- **Our design vs Nabokov:** the same two layers (line + notes) with three corrections —
  English grammar kept; the apparatus typed (cards = lexical meaning, alternatives =
  readings, notes = the rest); every rare word must be more exact, not merely rarer.
- **Jakobson (1959):** poetry is untranslatable, only creative transposition; paronomasia
  "reigns over poetic art" [V] — sound-sense play is what the line cannot fully carry.
- **Delabastita (1996):** eight strategies for wordplay. Our design allows only pun→pun in
  the line, and pun→non-pun in the line *with* editorial technique (alternatives, notes);
  it forbids pun→zero and any added wordplay.
- **Appiah (1993), "thick translation":** annotation locating the text in its context — the
  theory of the card and note layers.
- **Cassin (2004):** the untranslatable is "what one keeps on (not) translating" [V] — dharma,
  yoga, ātman, brahman: a stable rendering or retention plus a permanent card, never a final
  answer.
- **Buber–Rosenzweig / Everett Fox:** the *Leitwort* — a repeated source word or root stays
  one repeated target word so echoes are heard across passages.
- **Pound:** phanopoeia (image), melopoeia (sound), logopoeia (usage play); image translates
  well, sound and usage-play poorly [P] — a three-way audit of loss.

## 3. Sanskrit → English precedents

- **Sargeant (1979/2009):** Devanāgarī, transliteration, word-for-word English, grammatical
  analysis, vocabulary — the closest model for the card layer; no attempt at a beautiful
  line. Reviewers' faults unverified.
- **Edgerton (1944):** "exceptionally literal"; Prabhavananda–Isherwood set interpretation
  against it.
- **Jamison & Brereton, *Rigveda* (2014):** principles section p. 75 not read; criticised for
  too few notes — a literal line without notes fails.
- **Śleṣa** (Bronner, *Extreme Poetry*, 2010): double readings from different word splits of
  one string — the one class of loss structurally impossible to remove from the line.

## 4. What "lossless" can coherently mean

Losslessness belongs to the whole set of layers: every sense, relation and double value of
the Sanskrit must be recoverable from line + card + alternatives + notes; nothing may be
added that the Sanskrit does not support; the line carries as much as English grammar
allows.

- **Removable in the line:** lexical sense (an English word or compound of the same range);
  grammatical relations (case, agency, voice, compound relation); many double values (an
  English word or compound with the same ambiguity); repetition of key words and roots;
  the image.
- **Removable only across layers:** readings needing another word split (śleṣa);
  etymological and sound play; allusion and commentarial senses; metre; alternative parses
  where English must commit.
- **Named trade-offs:** crabbedness (Dryden, Wilson); euphony and grammar (Nabokov — the
  owner refuses the grammar cost); no mixing of directions (Schleiermacher); sound-meaning
  untranslatable (Jakobson); distinctiveness turning "queer" (Hopkins); a coinage stays a
  nonce word until defined and used consistently (Bauer, Downing).

## 5. Operational rules

**(a) Choosing a word.** Match the range of the Sanskrit word in context; prefer English with
the same ambiguity. A rare word only when more exact than any common one (Wilson's test),
reason on the card. Same sense → same English throughout; a real shift of sense changes the
English and the card says so. Among equally exact options, prefer native roots when they keep
the image or root play; keep the exact Latinate term where it is the term. Never fix in
English a distinction the Sanskrit leaves open.

**(b) Coining a compound.** Only when no English word carries the combined sense; head on the
right; intact attested morphemes; relation recoverable from context or card; where the
Sanskrit compound is deliberately ambiguous, an English form that keeps the ambiguity, with
the readings on the card; coherent metaphor (*nykrat*); defined on first card, then fixed in
form and sense; hyphenate on first use unless the solid form is established.

**(c) Building the line.** English grammar is a hard constraint. Within it, follow the
Sanskrit order of ideas, emphasis, first and last place. Keep repetitions and parallels.
Aim for English cadence carrying the śloka's weight and closure, never trading a sense for a
beat. Add nothing without a slot; grammar-only words are marked. Beauty from exact diction,
coherent image and cadence, never ornament.

**(d) Cards and notes.** Cards: full sense range, root, form, why this English, definition of
any coinage, the consistent rendering. Alternatives: other splits, other parses, compound
relations, commentarial senses with sources. Notes: sound and etymological play, allusion,
metre, context. The line never contains what cards and notes cannot justify; cards and notes
never hold a sense the line could have carried.

**(e) Testing for loss.** Back-mapping (every morpheme to a slot or a marked card/note entry);
sense inventory from the lexicon and commentaries, each sense recoverable, no added sense;
ambiguity never silently resolved; Pound audit of image, sound, usage-play; coinage check
(rebuildable from the card, head right, coherent, consistent); Wilson test for rare words;
grammar check; concordance check across the whole Gītā.

## 6. Ten principles ranked for this project

1. Losslessness applies to the whole set of layers.
2. Add nothing.
3. Never silently resolve an ambiguity the Sanskrit keeps.
4. Exact contextual meaning, within English grammar.
5. The same thing rendered the same way across the whole work (Leitwort).
6. Keep the Sanskrit's way of meaning: order, emphasis, parallelism, image.
7. A coinage must be legible from its parts, defined once, then fixed.
8. Rare words must be more exact, not merely more distinctive.
9. Name each loss by kind and give it a place.
10. Beauty is exact diction plus English cadence, never ornament.
